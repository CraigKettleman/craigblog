## Foundations Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q0a">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q0a: What is the role of the attention mechanism in a decoder-only Transformer? Why is it causal?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The attention mechanism allows each token to attend to (i.e., compute a weighted combination of) representations from other tokens. In a decoder-only Transformer, attention is causal (also called <em>autoregressive</em>): token <math alttext="t" display="inline"><semantics><mi>t</mi></semantics></math> can only attend to tokens <math alttext="1,\ldots,t" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>,</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">…</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></semantics></math>, never to future tokens <math alttext="t+1,\ldots,T" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mo>,</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">…</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>Why causal? Because the model generates text left-to-right. At inference time, future tokens literally do not exist yet. The causal mask during training simulates this constraint so that the model learns to predict each token using only its left context. Mathematically, the attention matrix is masked:</p><div class="hh-equation"><math alttext="\text{Attention}(Q,K,V)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}+M\right)V" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mtext>Attention</mtext><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>Q</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>K</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>V</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>=</mo><mrow><mpadded width="3.720em"><mtext>softmax</mtext></mpadded><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo>(</mo><mrow><mfrac><mrow><mi>Q</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msup><mi>K</mi><mo>⊤</mo></msup></mrow><msqrt><msub><mi>d</mi><mi>k</mi></msub></msqrt></mfrac><mo>+</mo><mi>M</mi></mrow><mo>)</mo></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>V</mi></mrow></mrow></semantics></math></div><p>where <math alttext="M_{ij}=-\infty" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>M</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>j</mi></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo>−</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">∞</mi></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> for <math alttext="j&gt;i" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>j</mi><mo>&gt;</mo><mi>i</mi></mrow></semantics></math> (future positions), forcing those attention weights to zero.</p><p>Practical implication: This enables the KV-cache optimization at inference—since past tokens’ keys and values never change, they can be cached and reused, reducing generation from <math alttext="O(T^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>T</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> to <math alttext="O(T)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>T</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> per new token.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q0b">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q0b: Explain Flash Attention. What problem does it solve and how?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard attention computes the full <math alttext="T\times T" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>T</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow></semantics></math> attention matrix, which requires <math alttext="O(T^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>T</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> memory and is memory-bandwidth bound—the GPU spends most of its time moving data between HBM (slow, large) and SRAM (fast, small), not doing actual computation.</p><p>Flash Attention’s insight: Never materialize the full attention matrix in HBM. Instead, tile the computation into blocks that fit in SRAM, compute attention <em>block by block</em> using an online softmax algorithm, and write only the final output to HBM.</p><p>Key techniques:</p><ol><li>Tiling: Split Q, K, V into blocks of size <math alttext="B_{r}\times B_{c}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>B</mi><mi>r</mi></msub><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><msub><mi>B</mi><mi>c</mi></msub></mrow></semantics></math> that fit in SRAM</li><li>Online softmax: Track running max and sum to compute softmax incrementally without the full row</li><li>Recomputation: In the backward pass, recompute attention from Q, K, V (cheap) rather than storing the <math alttext="T\times T" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>T</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow></semantics></math> matrix (expensive)</li></ol><p>Result: <math alttext="O(T)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>T</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> HBM memory (instead of <math alttext="O(T^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>T</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>), 2–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> wall-clock speedup, exact same numerical output (not an approximation).</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1–2 (LLM Architecture; Systems Foundations).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q0c">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q0c: What is the difference between SFT, RLHF, and DPO at a high level? When do you use each?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<ul><li>SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning): Train the model to imitate high-quality demonstrations. Loss: next-token prediction on curated data. Teaches <em>format</em> and <em>style</em>.</li><li>RLHF: Train a reward model from human preferences, then optimize the policy against it using RL (PPO). The model explores beyond the demonstration data. Teaches <em>what humans prefer</em>.</li><li>DPO: Skip the reward model. Directly optimize the policy on preference pairs <math alttext="(y_{w},y_{l})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></semantics></math> using a contrastive loss. Same goal as RLHF but simpler pipeline.</li></ul><p>Typical pipeline: SFT first (gives the model a good starting point), then RLHF or DPO (refines preferences). SFT alone tends to produce verbose, hedge-heavy responses. RLHF/DPO makes outputs more direct and aligned with human intent.</p><p>When to use each: SFT when you have gold-standard outputs. DPO when you have preference pairs but limited compute. RLHF (PPO) when you need maximum quality and can afford the infrastructure.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 5, 6, and 10 (PPO; DPO; SFT Best Practices).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q0d">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q0d: What is a reward model? How is it trained and what can go wrong?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>A reward model (RM) is a neural network that takes a (prompt, response) pair and outputs a scalar score indicating quality. It is trained on human preference data: given pairs <math alttext="(y_{w},y_{l})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></semantics></math> where <math alttext="y_{w}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub></semantics></math> is preferred, the RM learns to assign <math alttext="R(y_{w})&gt;R(y_{l})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>&gt;</mo><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>Training: Bradley-Terry loss: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}=-\log\sigma(R(y_{w})-R(y_{l}))" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ℒ</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo rspace="0.167em">−</mo><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo lspace="0.167em">⁡</mo><mrow><mi>σ</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>R</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Architecture: typically the same transformer as the policy, with the LM head replaced by a scalar projection.</p><p>What can go wrong:</p><ol><li>Reward hacking: The policy finds outputs that score high on the RM but are actually low quality (e.g., excessively long, repetitive, or containing specific phrases the RM was biased toward)</li><li>Distribution shift: The RM was trained on outputs from an earlier policy. As training progresses, the current policy generates out-of-distribution outputs the RM cannot score accurately</li><li>Label noise: Human annotators disagree, are tired, or apply inconsistent criteria. This noise propagates into RM predictions</li><li>Overconfidence: The RM assigns extreme scores to outputs it has never seen, providing misleading gradient signal</li></ol><p><em>Review: Chapter 9 (Reward Model Training).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q0e">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q0e: Explain the exploration-exploitation trade-off in RL. How does it manifest in LLM training?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>In RL, an agent must balance:</p><ul><li>Exploitation: choosing actions known to yield high reward (greedy behavior)</li><li>Exploration: trying new actions that might yield even higher reward (but might also fail)</li></ul><p>In LLM training: The policy is the language model. “Actions” are token choices. “Exploitation” means generating responses similar to what already scored well. “Exploration” means trying novel phrasings, structures, or reasoning paths.</p><p>How it manifests:</p><ul><li>Temperature during generation: Higher temperature = more exploration. GRPO uses temperature 1.0 to get diverse samples within each group.</li><li>KL penalty: Acts as an anti-exploration brake—prevents the policy from straying too far from the reference model. Without it, the policy might collapse to a single high-reward template (mode collapse).</li><li>Group sampling in GRPO: Generating <math alttext="G" display="inline"><semantics><mi>G</mi></semantics></math> responses per prompt explicitly explores the output space, then reinforces above-average responses.</li></ul><p>The tension: Too little exploration <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> the model gets stuck in local optima (always giving the same safe answer). Too much <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> training is unstable, quality fluctuates wildly.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 3 and 7 (Introduction to RL; GRPO).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q1: Explain PPO’s clipped objective. Why does it work better than vanilla PG?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Vanilla policy gradient: <math alttext="\nabla J=\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi(a|s)\cdot\hat{A}]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo rspace="0.167em">∇</mo><mi>J</mi></mrow><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>𝔼</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><mrow><mo rspace="0.167em">∇</mo><mi>log</mi></mrow><mo lspace="0.167em">⁡</mo><mi>π</mi></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>a</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>s</mi><mo rspace="0.055em" stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Problem: one lucky/unlucky sample can produce a huge gradient <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> policy jumps to a bad region <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> generates garbage <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> next gradient makes it worse <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> unrecoverable “death spiral.”</p><p>PPO’s solution: Clip the probability ratio <math alttext="r=\pi_{\text{new}}/\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>new</mtext></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> to <math alttext="[0.8,1.2]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mn>0.8</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>1.2</mn><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>Mechanics: For good actions (<math alttext="\hat{A}&gt;0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mo>&gt;</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>): objective is <math alttext="\min(r\hat{A},1.2\hat{A})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>min</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><mo>,</mo><mrow><mn>1.2</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Once <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math> exceeds 1.2, no further benefit — stops the policy from over-committing to one example. For bad actions (<math alttext="\hat{A}&lt;0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mo>&lt;</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>): objective is <math alttext="\min(r\hat{A},0.8\hat{A})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>min</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><mo>,</mo><mrow><mn>0.8</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Once <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math> drops below 0.8, penalty stops growing — prevents catastrophic forgetting.</p><p>Key insight: It’s a first-order approximation of TRPO’s KL constraint, but without expensive second-order optimization. Each update changes the policy by at most <math alttext="\pm" display="inline"><semantics><mo>±</mo></semantics></math>20%.</p><p>For LLMs specifically: The token-level ratio <math alttext="r_{t}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|y_{&lt;t})/\pi_{\text{old}}(y_{t}|y_{&lt;t})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>r</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> prevents any single token’s probability from changing too drastically, preserving coherent generation.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 5 (PPO).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q3: GRPO vs PPO — when would you choose each? What’s the trade-off?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>GRPO advantages:</p><ul><li>No value function needed: saves one model’s worth of memory and complexity</li><li>Simpler: fewer hyperparameters, more intuitive (above-mean = good, below-mean = bad)</li><li>Better for verifiable rewards: math/code where <math alttext="r\in\{0,1\}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>∈</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">{</mo><mrow><mn>0</mn><mo>,</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mo stretchy="false">}</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> gives crisp signal</li><li>DeepSeek-R1 proved it can teach emergent reasoning with just binary rewards</li></ul><p>PPO advantages:</p><ul><li>Per-token credit assignment: value function assigns reward to each token, not just sequence-level</li><li>More sample efficient: GAE uses value predictions to estimate advantage without generating <math alttext="G" display="inline"><semantics><mi>G</mi></semantics></math> samples</li><li>Better for nuanced rewards: when reward is continuous and varies significantly across tokens</li><li>More mature: battle-tested at OpenAI, Anthropic, etc.</li></ul><p>Rule of thumb: If rewards are verifiable (right/wrong) <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> GRPO. <details class="hh-quiz" id="q5">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q5: How do you prevent reward hacking? Give a layered defense strategy.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Detection signals: RM score rising but win-rate flat/declining. Response length growing monotonically. KL divergence <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 15. Diversity (unique n-grams) dropping. Reading high-reward outputs reveals exploits.</p><p>Layered defense (in priority order):</p><ol><li>KL penalty (primary): Adaptive controller targets KL <math alttext="\approx" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≈</mo></semantics></math> 6. If KL rises, <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math> increases automatically. Prevents drifting too far from reference.</li><li>Reward model ensemble (3–5 models): Use min or mean of scores. Individual models have different blind spots — exploits that fool one rarely fool all.</li><li>Length penalty: <math alttext="r^{\prime}=r-c\cdot\max(0,\text{length}-L_{\text{target}})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msup><mi>r</mi><mo>′</mo></msup><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>c</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mrow><mi>max</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0</mn><mo>,</mo><mrow><mtext>length</mtext><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>L</mi><mtext>target</mtext></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Prevents “just generate longer = higher score” exploit.</li><li>Periodic RM refresh: Every 2000 steps, generate data from current policy, relabel, add to RM training set. Closes the exploit as model finds it.</li><li>Win-rate based stopping: Track win-rate against SFT baseline. If RM score rises but win-rate stalls for 200+ steps, stop training. The model is exploiting, not improving.</li></ol><p>Post-detection recovery: Roll back to last “clean” checkpoint. Increase <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math> by 2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>. Add the discovered exploit to the RM’s negative examples.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 9 and 11 (Reward Model Training; System Architecture).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q6">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q6: Design an RLHF system for training a 70B model. Walk through every component.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>

This hides generation latency, achieving 1.3–1.5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> throughput vs monolithic.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 11 (System Architecture &amp; Infrastructure at Scale).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q7">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q7: How do you handle the generation bottleneck? Quantify the solutions.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Generation = 60–70% of total RLHF wall-clock time. Root cause: autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth bound (arithmetic intensity <math alttext="\approx 1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>≈</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math> FLOP/byte vs roofline of 156 FLOP/byte on A100).</p><p>Solutions ranked by impact:</p><p>1. Decouple gen from training (1.3–1.5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> end-to-end): Run generation on separate hardware, overlap with training. The single biggest architectural win.</p><p>2. vLLM with PagedAttention (2–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>): Eliminates 60–80% KV cache memory waste from internal fragmentation. Enables 3–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> larger batches = better bandwidth utilization.</p><p>3. Continuous batching (1.5–2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>): Don’t wait for longest sequence to finish. Start new sequences immediately in freed slots. Keeps GPUs busy.</p><p>4. Speculative decoding (2–3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>): 1B draft model proposes 5 tokens. 70B model verifies all 5 in one forward pass (parallel!). Accept 3–4 on average <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> 3–4 tokens per forward pass instead of 1.</p><p>5. INT8/FP8 for generation weights (2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>): Halves the 140GB weight read per token. Quality loss is minimal because (a) we’re sampling with temperature anyway, and (b) only generation uses INT8, training stays BF16.</p><p>6. CUDA graphs + kernel fusion (1.1–1.3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>): Eliminate Python/CUDA launch overhead. Fuse layernorm+attention+MLP into fewer kernels.</p><p>Combined: <math alttext="1.5\times 3\times 1.5\times 2.5\times 2\times 1.2=40\times" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>1.5</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>3</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>1.5</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2.5</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>1.2</mn><mo>=</mo><mn>40</mn><mo lspace="0.222em">×</mo></mrow></semantics></math> over naive. In practice, diminishing returns limit total to <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>10–20<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> over naive implementation.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 2 and 11 (Systems Foundations; System Architecture).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q8">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q8: Explain weight synchronization in a decoupled system. How much staleness can you tolerate?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The problem: Generation cluster uses policy weights to produce responses. Training cluster updates those weights. They’re on different hardware. How to keep them in sync?</p><p>Why perfect sync is wasteful: Full sync of 70B BF16 = 140GB. At InfiniBand 400Gb/s (50GB/s): 2.8s per sync. If you sync every step (every 50–90s), you spend 3–5% of time on weight transfer. Acceptable, but unnecessary.</p><p>Staleness tolerance analysis:</p><ul><li>Per-step policy change: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>0.1% (measured by mean param delta)</li><li>50 steps: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5% cumulative drift</li><li>PPO clip range: handles up to 20% probability ratio deviation</li><li>Empirical: 50-step staleness <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math><math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>2% quality degradation (measured by win-rate)</li></ul><p>Production strategy:</p><ol><li>Every 50 training steps: push full BF16 checkpoint to shared store (2.8s transfer)</li><li>Generation cluster: non-blocking weight reload between batches</li><li>Delta compression (optional): Only send changed parameters (INT8 delta <math alttext="\approx" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≈</mo></semantics></math> 5GB), apply as offset. 10<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> less bandwidth.</li><li>For very large scale (256+ GPUs): streaming sync — continuously send small chunks in background. Average staleness: 5–10 steps.</li></ol><p>Important subtlety: Log-probs computed during generation use stale weights. The PPO ratio <math alttext="\pi_{\text{new}}/\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>new</mtext></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow></semantics></math> computes <math alttext="\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></semantics></math> using these stale log-probs. This is fine because PPO is designed for off-policy corrections.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 11 (System Architecture &amp; Infrastructure at Scale).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q9">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q9: How would you make this fault-tolerant at 512 GPUs?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>At 512 GPUs, MTBF is 4–8 hours. A 5-day training run will see 15–30 failures.</p><p>Architecture-level resilience:</p><ul><li>Generation cluster = stateless. Auto-delete older ones.</li></ul><p>Detection and recovery flow:</p><ol><li>NCCL collective timeout (60s) or heartbeat miss (10s) <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> failure detected.</li><li>Identify failed node(s) via NVML health check.</li><li>Option A (fast, <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>2 min): Torch Elastic shrinks world size, redistributes shards, continue with <math alttext="N-1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math> nodes. Request replacement in background.</li><li>Option B (clean, <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5 min): Bring up replacement node, rebuild process group, load last checkpoint, resume.</li><li>Experience buffer is persisted — no regeneration needed.</li></ol><p>Prevention: Pre-screening stress test (GEMM, memory, NVLink). ECC error monitoring (preemptive migration if errors spike). Hot spare nodes (pre-loaded with environment). Dual-rail InfiniBand for network redundancy.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 11 (System Architecture &amp; Infrastructure at Scale).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q10">

## Practical and Debugging Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q11">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q11: Reward score is increasing but model quality is declining. Diagnose and fix.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Classic reward hacking / Goodhart’s Law.</p><p>Diagnostic protocol:</p><ol><li>Check response length: Plot mean length over training. Growing monotonically? = Length exploit (RM gives higher scores to longer responses).</li><li>Check KL divergence: <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>15? = Policy has diverged too far from reference. Lost capabilities.</li><li>Check diversity: Unique trigrams per response. Dropping? = Mode collapse (repeating same high-reward pattern).</li><li>Manual inspection: Read 20 highest-reward responses. What pattern do they share? (e.g., all start with “Great question!”, all use bullet points, excessive hedging).</li><li>Win-rate: Evaluate against SFT baseline on held-out prompts. If flat/declining while RM rises = confirmed exploit.</li></ol><p>Immediate fixes:</p><ul><li>Roll back to last checkpoint where win-rate was improving</li><li>Increase <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math> by 2–3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> (stronger KL penalty)</li><li>Add explicit length penalty: <math alttext="r^{\prime}=r-0.001\cdot\max(0,\text{len}-500)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msup><mi>r</mi><mo>′</mo></msup><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>−</mo><mrow><mn>0.001</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mrow><mi>max</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0</mn><mo>,</mo><mrow><mtext>len</mtext><mo>−</mo><mn>500</mn></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math></li></ul><p>Structural fixes (prevent recurrence):</p><ul><li>RM ensemble: Train 3–5 RMs on different data splits. Use min or mean. Exploits are model-specific.</li><li>RM refresh: Every 2000 steps, generate from current policy, get human labels, retrain RM.</li><li>Multi-objective reward: Combine helpfulness + harmlessness + conciseness with separate RMs.</li><li>Early stopping on win-rate (not RM score). The metric you optimize should differ from training signal.</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapters 9 and 11 (Reward Model Training; System Architecture).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q12">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q12: How do you decide the prompt distribution for RL training?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Prompt quality is the most underrated factor in RLHF. Bad prompts = no learning signal.</p><p>Composition (my default mix):</p><ul><li>40% real user traffic (represents actual use cases)</li><li>30% synthetic (LLM-generated, fills gaps in coverage — rare topics, edge cases)</li><li>20% curriculum (graduated difficulty — start easy, increase complexity as model improves)</li><li>10% adversarial (red-team prompts, jailbreak attempts, ambiguous instructions)</li></ul><p>Critical: The Goldilocks Filter:</p><ol><li>For each candidate prompt, generate 4–8 responses with current model.</li><li>Score with RM. Compute pass rate (fraction above threshold).</li><li>Keep only prompts with 20–80% pass rate:<span class="hh-tag">•</span><math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>20%: Too hard. Model almost always fails <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> all negative advantages <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> no useful gradient.<span class="hh-tag">•</span><math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>80%: Too easy. Model almost always succeeds <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> all positive advantages <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> no contrast.<span class="hh-tag">•</span>20–80%: Perfect. Mix of successes and failures <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> clear signal about what works.</li><li>Re-filter every 500 training steps (model improves, difficulty distribution shifts).</li></ol><p>Topic diversity: Ensure no single topic dominates (<math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>10% per category). Use embedding clustering to verify coverage. Otherwise the model over-optimizes for dominant topics.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 9 (GRPO; Reward Model Training).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q13">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q13: LoRA vs full fine-tuning for RLHF. When would you use each?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation, <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math>=64, <math alttext="\alpha" display="inline"><semantics><mi>α</mi></semantics></math>=16):</p><ul><li>Trainable params: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>0.2% of model (200M for 70B)</li><li>Memory savings: No separate reference model needed (base model = reference)! Saves 140GB.</li><li>Stability: Inherently more stable (low-rank constraint limits how far policy can drift)</li><li>Speed: Faster per-step (fewer params to update), but may need more steps</li><li>Quality ceiling: 90–95% of full FT quality typically</li></ul><p>Full fine-tuning:</p><ul><li>All parameters updated. Maximum expressiveness.</li><li>Needs separate reference model copy (140GB for 70B). Or very frequent checkpointing to “anchor.”</li><li>Higher risk of catastrophic forgetting. Need lower LR (<math alttext="3\times" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>3</mn><mo lspace="0.222em">×</mo></mrow></semantics></math> lower than LoRA) and stronger <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math>.</li><li>Better when: large distributional shift needed (new language, very different style), LoRA hits capacity limit.</li></ul><p>My decision framework:</p><ol><li>Start with LoRA (<math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math>=64). It’s 3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> cheaper and more stable.</li><li>Monitor gradient norms on LoRA matrices. If consistently <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>1.0 (high relative to parameter count): LoRA is capacity-limited.</li><li>Switch to full FT only when: win-rate plateaus AND gradient analysis suggests capacity limitation.</li><li>For full FT: use <math alttext="\text{LR}/3" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mtext>LR</mtext><mo>/</mo><mn>3</mn></mrow></semantics></math>, <math alttext="\beta\times 2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>β</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></semantics></math>, more frequent checkpointing, and early stopping based on win-rate.</li></ol><p>Hybrid: LoRA for alignment/safety (small behavioral shift) + Full FT for capabilities/reasoning (large shift needed).</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 10 (LLM Architecture; SFT Best Practices).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q14">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q14: Process Reward Models (PRM) vs Outcome Reward Models (ORM). Design a PRM system.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>ORM: Scores the final answer only. “Is the response good overall?” Simple but can’t identify <em>where</em> reasoning went wrong.</p><p>PRM: Scores each intermediate step. “Is step 3 of this derivation correct?” Much more informative but harder to train.</p><p>PRM advantages for reasoning:</p><ul><li>Identifies exactly where reasoning fails (step-level credit assignment)</li><li>Enables tree search: expand only branches with high step scores</li><li>Less reward hacking: can’t get high score from wrong steps + lucky final answer</li><li>PRM + best-of-N beats ORM + best-of-N by 10–20% on MATH benchmarks</li></ul><p>Training a PRM:</p><ol><li>Data collection (Monte Carlo approach):<span class="hh-tag">•</span>For each problem, generate reasoning trace step by step<span class="hh-tag">•</span>At each step <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math>, complete the solution <math alttext="M" display="inline"><semantics><mi>M</mi></semantics></math> times (<math alttext="M=32" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>M</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>32</mn></mrow></semantics></math>) from that point<span class="hh-tag">•</span>Step score = fraction of completions that reach correct answer<span class="hh-tag">•</span>Steps where score drops significantly = the “mistake” steps</li><li>Labeling: Step is “correct” if its completion rate <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 50%, “incorrect” if <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math> 20%.</li><li>Model: Same architecture as base model + classification head per token position. Train with binary cross-entropy on step labels.</li><li>Inference: Score each step. If any step scores <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>0.3, flag the trace as flawed.</li></ol><p>Using PRM in RLHF: Per-token rewards from PRM feed directly into GAE. Each token gets immediate feedback, not just end-of-sequence. This dramatically improves credit assignment for long reasoning chains.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 9 and 13 (Reward Model Training; RL for Large Reasoning Models).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q15">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q15: How do you evaluate whether RL actually improved the model?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Multi-faceted evaluation (no single metric captures “quality”):</p><p>1. Win-rate (most important, most reliable):</p><ul><li>500+ diverse prompts. LLM judge (GPT-4 or Claude) picks winner in blind A/B comparison vs SFT baseline.</li><li>Target: <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>55% win-rate = meaningful improvement. <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>65% = strong improvement.</li><li>Use position-debiasing (swap A/B order, average). Report confidence intervals.</li></ul><p>2. Capability benchmarks (regression detection):</p><ul><li>MMLU (knowledge), HumanEval (code), MATH (reasoning), MT-Bench (multi-turn).</li><li>Any <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>2% drop = concerning alignment tax. Investigate which categories degraded.</li></ul><p>3. Category-specific evals:</p><ul><li>Safety: refusal rate on harmful prompts (should increase)</li><li>Truthfulness: TruthfulQA score (should increase or stay flat)</li><li>Helpfulness: task completion rate on instruction-following benchmarks</li></ul><p>4. Distributional metrics:</p><ul><li>Response length distribution (shouldn’t shift dramatically)</li><li>Vocabulary diversity (unique tokens per response)</li><li>Format compliance (if trained for specific format)</li></ul><p>5. Human evaluation (gold standard, expensive):</p><ul><li>Blind A/B with 3+ skilled annotators per example. Inter-annotator agreement <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 70%.</li><li>Only for final model selection, not during training (too slow/expensive).</li></ul><p>Red flags: RM score up + win-rate flat = reward hacking, not real improvement. Win-rate up + benchmarks down = alignment tax too high, reduce RL strength.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 14 (LLM Evaluation).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q16">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q16: Describe the reward model training pipeline end-to-end.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Phase 1 — Data Generation:</p><ul><li>Collect 50K–100K diverse prompts (real traffic + synthetic)</li><li>Generate 4–8 responses per prompt at varying temperatures (0.3, 0.7, 1.0) and from multiple models (diversity is key — if all responses are similar, preferences are uninformative)</li><li>Total: 200K–800K candidate responses</li></ul><p>Phase 2 — Preference Collection:</p><ul><li>Option A (expensive, best quality): Human annotators compare pairs. 3 annotators per pair. Cost: $2–5 per comparison.</li><li>Option B (cheap, 85–90% agreement with humans): LLM judge (GPT-4/Claude). 10<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> cheaper. Good for scale.</li><li>Format: (prompt, chosen response, rejected response). Pairs with annotator disagreement (<math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>70% agreement) are discarded.</li><li>Final dataset: 100K–500K pairs.</li></ul><p>Phase 3 — Training:</p><ul><li>Architecture: Same as base LLM + scalar head (one regression output per sequence).</li><li>Loss: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}=-\mathbb{E}[\log\sigma(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}))]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ℒ</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>𝔼</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo lspace="0.167em">⁡</mo><mrow><mi>σ</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> (Bradley-Terry).</li><li>Training: 1 epoch only! RMs overfit extremely fast. Validation accuracy 68–75% is good (higher often means overfitting to annotation artifacts).</li><li>Tricks: Center rewards around 0 (subtract running mean). Check for length bias (if correlation between length and score <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 0.3, add length penalty to training).</li></ul><p>Phase 4 — Validation:</p><ul><li>Hold-out preference pairs: accuracy should be 68–75%.</li><li>Agreement with humans on new data: <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 80%.</li><li>Length bias check: correlation between response length and RM score should be <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math> 0.2.</li><li>Consistency check: same prompt, paraphrased responses should get similar scores.</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapter 9 (Reward Model Training).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q17">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q17: What happens when KL divergence explodes? Root cause and fix.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>What KL measures: Average log-ratio between current policy and reference: <math alttext="D_{\text{KL}}=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_{\theta}}[\log(\pi_{\theta}(y|x)/\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x))]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>D</mi><mtext>KL</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>𝔼</mi><mrow><mi>y</mi><mo>∼</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub></mrow></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>y</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>ref</mtext></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>y</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. KL=0 means identical to reference. KL=10 means the policy puts 10 nats more probability on its preferred outputs.</p><p>Healthy range: 3–10 during training. Slowly increasing is fine. Sudden spike = problem.</p><p>Root causes of KL explosion:</p><ol><li>Learning rate too high: Policy takes giant step, diverges from reference. Fix: reduce LR by 2–5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>.</li><li>Reward hacking: Found an exploit that gets high reward far from reference behavior. Fix: increase <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math>, add RM ensemble.</li><li>Mode collapse: Policy concentrates on one response template. KL is high at that template, low everywhere else. Fix: increase entropy bonus, increase temperature.</li><li>Bad batch: One unlucky batch with extreme advantages pushed the policy. Fix: gradient clipping, reduce mini-batch size.</li><li>Value function diverged: Wrong advantage estimates cause wrong updates. Fix: reduce value function LR, or switch to GRPO (no value function).</li></ol><p>Recovery protocol:</p><ol><li>Detect: KL <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 15 for 50+ steps, or KL jumps <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>5 in one step.</li><li>Immediate: Load last clean checkpoint (KL <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math> 10).</li><li>Adjust: Reduce LR by 50%. Increase <math alttext="\beta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>β</mi></semantics></math> by 2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>. Lower cliprange to 0.1.</li><li>Resume: Monitor closely for first 200 steps.</li></ol><p><em>Review: Chapters 5 and 7 (PPO; GRPO).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q18">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q18: Compare monolithic vs decoupled RLHF architectures. When does each make sense?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Monolithic (TRL default: single process, all models on same GPUs):</p><ul><li>Pros: Simple code. No distributed systems complexity. Easy debugging.</li><li>Cons: GPUs idle 60% of time (compute idle during gen, bandwidth idle during training). Doesn’t scale past <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>16 GPUs efficiently. All models compete for same memory.</li><li>Use when: Model <math alttext="\leq" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≤</mo></semantics></math> 13B, single node, research/prototyping.</li></ul><p>Semi-decoupled (vLLM gen + FSDP training, same cluster):</p><ul><li>Pros: Better utilization (gen and training can partially overlap). Scales to 64 GPUs.</li><li>Cons: Still shares hardware, can’t optimize independently. More complex than monolithic.</li><li>Use when: 13B–70B, 2–8 nodes, production experiments.</li></ul><p>Fully decoupled (separate clusters connected by queues):</p><ul><li>Pros: Each cluster optimized for its workload. Gen scales independently from training. Gen cluster is stateless (trivial fault tolerance). Scales to hundreds of GPUs.</li><li>Cons: Distributed systems complexity. Weight staleness. Queue management. Network overhead.</li><li>Use when: <math alttext="\geq" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≥</mo></semantics></math> 70B production training. Need scale, fault tolerance, high utilization.</li></ul><p>The key insight: Generation is bandwidth-bound, training is compute-bound. Same hardware can’t optimize for both. Decoupling lets gen nodes have: more memory bandwidth, INT8 weights, large batch. Training nodes have: full BF16 precision, Flash Attention, FSDP sharding.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 11 (System Architecture &amp; Infrastructure at Scale).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q19">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q19: How do you set up curriculum learning for RL training?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Curriculum = gradually increasing difficulty so the model learns progressively.</p><p>Why it matters: If you throw the hardest prompts at a weak model, it gets all-negative rewards <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> no learning signal (everything is equally bad). If you start easy, the model develops basic capabilities, then builds on them.</p><p>Implementation:</p><ol><li>Difficulty scoring: Rate each prompt by current model’s pass rate (from Goldilocks filtering). Easy = <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>80% pass, Medium = 30–80%, Hard = <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>30%.</li><li>Schedule: Steps 0–1000: 70% easy, 20% medium, 10% hard. Steps 1000–5000: 30% easy, 50% medium, 20% hard. Steps 5000+: 10% easy, 40% medium, 50% hard.</li><li>Dynamic adjustment: Every 500 steps, re-evaluate difficulty distribution. Prompts the model has “mastered” (pass rate <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math> 95%) get retired. New harder prompts are introduced.</li><li>For GRPO specifically: Curriculum ensures groups always have a mix of successes and failures. Without curriculum, hard prompts give all-zero groups (useless).</li></ol><p>Evidence: DeepSeek-R1 used implicit curriculum — starting with easy math/code problems, the model developed basic reasoning, then solved progressively harder problems without explicit scheduling.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 12 (GRPO; LLM Agentic Training).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q20">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q20: You have a budget of 64 A100-80GB GPUs and need to RL-train a 70B model. Design the allocation.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>8 nodes <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> 8 GPUs = 64 total. Need to split across generation, scoring, and training.</p><p>My allocation:</p><ul><li>Generation: 24 GPUs (3 nodes). 6 vLLM instances with TP=4. INT8 weights = 70GB/model, leaves room for KV cache. Continuous batching, batch <math alttext="\approx" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≈</mo></semantics></math> 128 total.</li><li>Scoring: 8 GPUs (1 node). RM (INT8, TP=4) + Reference model (INT8, TP=4) on same node. Or share 4 GPUs with TP=4 alternating between RM and ref.</li><li>Training: 32 GPUs (4 nodes). FSDP across all 32. Each GPU holds <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>70B/32 = 2.2GB params + optimizer fraction. Plenty of headroom for activations. Gradient checkpointing for safety.</li></ul><p>Expected throughput:</p><ul><li>Generation: 6 instances <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math><math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>80 responses/min = 480 responses/min</li><li>Training: Batch of 128, one step every <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>15s (training only, no gen wait)</li><li>Overlap: While training step <math alttext="N" display="inline"><semantics><mi>N</mi></semantics></math> (15s), generation produces <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>120 responses for step <math alttext="N+1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. Perfect pipeline.</li></ul><p>Bottleneck analysis: Generation takes <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>45s for 128 responses. Training takes <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>15s. Scoring takes <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5s. Generation is bottleneck. Could move 8 GPUs from training to gen (40 gen, 24 training) to balance, but then training is bottleneck. Current allocation is near-optimal.</p><p>Alternative if memory-tight: Move scoring onto training nodes (time-share: score during gen, train during training). Saves 8 GPUs. Slightly worse pipelining but works.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 2 and 11 (Systems Foundations; System Architecture).</em></p>

## GRPO Variants and Advanced RL Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q21">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q21: What is DAPO and how does it improve over standard GRPO?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>DAPO (Dynamic Adaptive Policy Optimization) introduces 5 key modifications:</p><p>1. Clip-Higher (asymmetric clipping): Standard PPO/GRPO clips both directions equally at <math alttext="\epsilon=0.2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ϵ</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0.2</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. DAPO uses <math alttext="\epsilon_{\text{low}}=0.2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>ϵ</mi><mtext>low</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>0.2</mn></mrow></semantics></math> but <math alttext="\epsilon_{\text{high}}=0.28" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>ϵ</mi><mtext>high</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>0.28</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. This allows the model to <em>increase</em> good action probabilities more aggressively while still restricting how much it suppresses bad ones. Intuition: exploration needs more room than exploitation.</p><p>2. Overlong Filtering: If a response hits the max length limit (truncated, no EOS token), it’s masked entirely from the loss. Rationale: truncated responses contain no natural stopping signal — training on them teaches the model that “stopping mid-sentence” is acceptable.</p><p>3. Token-level Loss: Loss is normalized by total token count across all sequences, not by number of sequences. This prevents longer sequences from dominating the gradient.</p><p>4. Soft Overlong Punishment: Instead of binary truncation filtering, apply a gradual penalty as responses approach max length. <math alttext="r_{\text{soft}}=-c\cdot\max(0,\text{len}-L_{\text{soft}})/(L_{\text{max}}-L_{\text{soft}})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>soft</mtext></msub><mo rspace="0em">=</mo><mo lspace="0em">−</mo><mi>c</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mi>max</mi><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mn>0</mn><mo>,</mo><mtext>len</mtext><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>L</mi><mtext>soft</mtext></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>L</mi><mtext>max</mtext></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>L</mi><mtext>soft</mtext></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>5. Dynamic Sampling: Resample prompts during training to ensure each batch has a mix of success/failure (not yet in TRL).</p><p>When to use: Large-scale reasoning RL where you need maximum exploration and long completions (32K+ tokens). The asymmetric clipping is particularly valuable.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 8 (GRPO; Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q22">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q22: Explain the vLLM train-inference mismatch. Why does it happen and how do TIS/MIS fix it?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The problem: When using vLLM for generation and a training framework (DeepSpeed/FSDP) for updates, the same model with the same weights produces <em>different</em> token probabilities. This happens because:</p><ul><li>Different numerical kernels (vLLM uses custom CUDA kernels optimized for throughput)</li><li>Different attention implementations (Flash Attention in training vs PagedAttention in vLLM)</li><li>Different precision handling (FP8/INT8 in vLLM vs BF16 in training)</li><li>Batching differences affecting layer normalization numerics</li></ul><p>This silently breaks PPO’s on-policy assumption: we compute the ratio <math alttext="\pi_{\theta}/\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow></semantics></math> using <math alttext="\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></semantics></math> from vLLM but <math alttext="\pi_{\theta}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub></semantics></math> from the training framework. The ratio is wrong from step zero!</p><p>TIS (Truncated Importance Sampling): Correct the gradient by multiplying by <math alttext="\min(\pi_{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{inference}},C)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>min</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>train</mtext></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>inference</mtext></msub></mrow><mo>,</mo><mi>C</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. The <math alttext="\min" display="inline"><semantics><mi>min</mi></semantics></math> with cap <math alttext="C" display="inline"><semantics><mi>C</mi></semantics></math> prevents extreme corrections from destabilizing training. Typical <math alttext="C=2.0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>C</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2.0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>MIS (Masked Importance Sampling): More aggressive — simply discard any token where <math alttext="\pi_{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{inference}}&gt;C" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>train</mtext></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>inference</mtext></msub></mrow><mo>&gt;</mo><mi>C</mi></mrow></semantics></math>. Zero contribution to gradient. Prevents any badly-estimated token from affecting the update.</p><p>Sequence vs Token level: Sequence-level IS is theoretically correct (unbiased); token-level IS is biased but lower variance. In practice, sequence-level with truncation works best.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 11 (GRPO; System Architecture).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q23">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q23: GSPO vs GRPO — what’s the fundamental difference and when does it matter?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>GRPO: Computes importance ratio <em>per token</em>: <math alttext="w_{i,t}=\pi_{\theta}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,&lt;t})/\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i,t}|q,o_{i,&lt;t})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>w</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mrow><mi>q</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mrow><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>,</mo><mphantom></mphantom></mrow><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mrow><mi>q</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mrow><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>,</mo><mphantom></mphantom></mrow><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>, then clips each token independently.</p><p>GSPO: Computes importance ratio at the <em>sequence level</em>: <math alttext="s_{i}(\theta)=(\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)/\pi_{\text{old}}(o_{i}|q))^{1/|o_{i}|}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>s</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>θ</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>=</mo><msup><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>q</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>q</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">|</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">|</mo></mrow></mrow></msup></mrow></semantics></math> — the geometric mean of token probabilities. Clips this single sequence-level ratio.</p><p>Why it matters: GRPO’s per-token clipping treats each token as independent, but in language they’re deeply correlated. A small per-token change early in the sequence compounds exponentially over many tokens. GSPO captures this by looking at the full sequence probability.</p><p>Length normalization: The <math alttext="1/|o_{i}|" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">|</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">|</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> exponent ensures fair comparison across different-length sequences. Without it, longer sequences would always have lower probability ratios.</p><p>When to use GSPO: When training goes off-policy (<code>steps_per_generation &gt; 1</code> or <code>num_iterations &gt; 1</code>). If fully on-policy (ratio <math alttext="\approx 1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>≈</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math>), GRPO and GSPO are equivalent.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 8 (GRPO; Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q24">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q24: The paper “It Takes Two” shows G=2 matches G=16. How is that possible?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The key insight is that GRPO’s effectiveness doesn’t come from accurate advantage estimation (which would need large <math alttext="G" display="inline"><semantics><mi>G</mi></semantics></math>), but from an implicit contrastive objective.</p><p>With <math alttext="G=2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></semantics></math> and binary rewards (one correct, one wrong): <math alttext="\hat{A}_{\text{correct}}=+1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mtext>correct</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>, <math alttext="\hat{A}_{\text{wrong}}=-1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mtext>wrong</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> (after normalization). The loss becomes: increase probability of correct response, decrease probability of wrong response. This is essentially a DPO-style contrastive loss!</p><p>Why large <math alttext="G" display="inline"><semantics><mi>G</mi></semantics></math> doesn’t help much: The normalized advantage <math alttext="\hat{A}_{i}=(r_{i}-\mu)/\sigma" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msub><mi>r</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><mi>μ</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo>/</mo><mi>σ</mi></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> already creates a contrast between good and bad. More samples give a better <math alttext="\mu" display="inline"><semantics><mi>μ</mi></semantics></math> estimate, but the gradient direction is dominated by the <em>contrast</em> between best and worst, not the mean accuracy.</p><p>Compute savings: <math alttext="G=2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></semantics></math> means 8<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> less generation compute than <math alttext="G=16" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>16</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. Since generation is 60% of training time, this translates to <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> faster training.</p><p>Caveat: Works best when pass rate is 30–70%. If pass rate is very low (<math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>10%), <math alttext="G=2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></semantics></math> often gives two failures (no signal). Need larger <math alttext="G" display="inline"><semantics><mi>G</mi></semantics></math> for hard problems.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 7 (GRPO).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q25">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q25: What is SAPO and how does its soft gating differ from hard clipping?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard PPO/GRPO uses hard clipping: <math alttext="\text{clip}(r,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mtext>clip</mtext><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>r</mi><mo>,</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>ϵ</mi></mrow><mo>,</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>+</mo><mi>ϵ</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. At the boundary, the gradient suddenly drops to zero. This creates a “dead zone” where the model receives no learning signal.</p><p>SAPO replaces this with a smooth sigmoid gate: the gradient is gradually attenuated as the ratio moves away from 1, never suddenly zeroed out. It uses asymmetric temperatures:</p><ul><li><math alttext="\tau_{+}=1.0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>τ</mi><mo>+</mo></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>1.0</mn></mrow></semantics></math> for positive advantages (standard attenuation)</li><li><math alttext="\tau_{-}=1.05" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>τ</mi><mo>−</mo></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>1.05</mn></mrow></semantics></math> for negative advantages (slightly more aggressive attenuation for suppression)</li></ul><p>Benefits: (1) No “cliff” in gradient landscape. (2) Tokens slightly outside the clip range still contribute (attenuated, not zeroed). (3) More stable optimization trajectory. (4) Sequence-coherent — considers the full sequence context.</p><p>Trade-off: Slightly less restrictive trust region than hard clipping, so requires careful temperature tuning. But more robust to hyperparameter choices overall.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 8 (GRPO; Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

## DPO Extensions Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q26">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q26: Compare f-DPO divergence choices. When would you use forward KL vs JS vs reverse KL?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard DPO uses reverse KL implicitly (<math alttext="D_{\text{KL}}[\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>D</mi><mtext>KL</mtext></msub><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0.167em">∥</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>ref</mtext></msub><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>):</p><ul><li>Reverse KL (default): Mode-seeking. The policy concentrates probability where the reference is high. Avoids generating text the reference wouldn’t. Good for safety (conservative).</li><li>Forward KL: Mass-covering. The policy tries to cover all modes of the reference, even low-probability ones. Good for diversity but can generate low-quality outputs.</li><li>Jensen-Shannon: Symmetric compromise between forward and reverse. Balanced mode-coverage and mode-seeking. Often best for general alignment.</li><li>Alpha-divergence (<math alttext="\alpha=0.5" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0.5</mn></mrow></semantics></math>): Interpolates between forward (<math alttext="\alpha=0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>) and reverse (<math alttext="\alpha=1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math>). Tunable.</li></ul><p>Practical recommendation: Start with reverse KL (standard DPO). If the model is too conservative (won’t try creative solutions), switch to JS divergence. If diversity is critical (creative writing, brainstorming), try forward KL.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 6 and 8 (DPO; Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q27">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q27: Your DPO preference data has 15% label noise. What do you do?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Three options in order of sophistication:</p><p>1. Robust DPO (best for known noise rate): Analytically debiases the loss: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}_{\text{robust}}=\frac{(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(y_{w},y_{l})-\varepsilon\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(y_{l},y_{w})}{1-2\varepsilon}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>ℒ</mi><mtext>robust</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mfrac><mrow><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mi>ε</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>ℒ</mi><mtext>DPO</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>ε</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>ℒ</mi><mtext>DPO</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>ε</mi></mrow></mrow></mfrac></mrow></semantics></math>. Set <math alttext="\varepsilon=0.15" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ε</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0.15</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. This provably recovers the clean DPO objective in expectation. TRL: <code>loss_type="robust", label_smoothing=0.15</code>.</p><p>2. IPO (best when noise rate unknown): Squared loss with target margin. Mislabeled pairs have bounded influence (the squared loss doesn’t diverge). More robust to arbitrary noise patterns without needing to know <math alttext="\varepsilon" display="inline"><semantics><mi>ε</mi></semantics></math>. TRL: <code>loss_type="ipo"</code>.</p><p>3. TR-DPO (best for distribution shift): Updates the reference model via EMA during training. Even if early data is noisy, the evolving reference helps the model self-correct. TRL: <code>sync_ref_model=True, ref_model_mixup_alpha=0.6</code>.</p><p>Data-side fixes: (1) Filter pairs with <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>70% inter-annotator agreement. (2) Use RM to score pairs; discard those where RM disagrees with label. (3) Active learning: re-label the most uncertain pairs.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 6 and 8 (DPO; Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q28">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q28: What is SimPO and why is being reference-free advantageous?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>SimPO uses the average log-probability of a response as an implicit reward signal: <math alttext="r(x,y)=\frac{1}{|y|}\sum_{t}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{t}|x,y_{&lt;t})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><mi>y</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>=</mo><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mrow><mo stretchy="false">|</mo><mi>y</mi><mo stretchy="false">|</mo></mrow></mfrac><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><msub><mo>∑</mo><mi>t</mi></msub><mrow><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo lspace="0.167em">⁡</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>θ</mi></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mrow><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> — no reference model needed.</p><p>The loss adds a target margin <math alttext="\gamma" display="inline"><semantics><mi>γ</mi></semantics></math>: the chosen response should have average log-prob at least <math alttext="\gamma" display="inline"><semantics><mi>γ</mi></semantics></math> higher than rejected.</p><p>Why reference-free matters:</p><ol><li>Memory: No reference model = 70–140GB saved for 70B models. Can train larger models on same hardware.</li><li>Simplicity: No need to manage/load/serve a second model copy.</li><li>No stale reference: DPO’s reference becomes increasingly irrelevant as training progresses. SimPO doesn’t have this problem.</li><li>Length normalization built in: The <math alttext="1/|y|" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">|</mo><mi>y</mi><mo stretchy="false">|</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> naturally prevents length bias (DPO needs explicit handling).</li></ol><p>Trade-off: Without a reference anchor, the model has more freedom to collapse or drift. The <math alttext="\gamma" display="inline"><semantics><mi>γ</mi></semantics></math> margin and length normalization partially mitigate this, but SimPO can be less stable than DPO for aggressive training.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 8 (Preference Optimization Variants).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q29">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q29: Explain Iterative RPO. Why combine DPO with NLL loss for reasoning?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard DPO for reasoning has a subtle failure mode: it learns to <em>discriminate</em> (assign higher implicit reward to correct traces) but doesn’t necessarily learn to <em>generate</em> them.</p><p>Why: DPO’s gradient pushes chosen probability up and rejected probability down. But the chosen response might be so different from what the model would generate that increasing its probability doesn’t teach the model how to produce similar reasoning patterns.</p><p>RPO’s fix: Add a negative log-likelihood (NLL/SFT) loss on the chosen response: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}+\alpha\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(y_{w})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ℒ</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>ℒ</mi><mtext>DPO</mtext></msub><mo>+</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><msub><mi>ℒ</mi><mtext>NLL</mtext></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>The NLL term explicitly trains the model to generate the winning response step by step. The DPO term ensures it also learns to avoid the losing response. Combined: the model learns both “how to reason correctly” (NLL) and “what to avoid” (DPO).</p><p>Iterative: Generate responses <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> check correctness <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> create pairs <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> train with RPO <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> repeat. Each iteration the model gets better at generating correct reasoning, creating higher-quality training data for the next round.</p><p>TRL: <code>loss_type=["sigmoid", "sft"], loss_weights=[1.0, 1.0]</code></p><p><em>Review: Chapters 8 and 13 (Preference Optimization Variants; RL for Large Reasoning Models).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

## GPU Architecture and Hardware Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q30">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q30: Explain the GPU memory hierarchy. Why does it matter for LLM inference?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>From fastest to slowest:</p><ol><li>Registers: Per-thread, <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>256 KB/SM. Instant access (0 cycles latency).</li><li>SRAM (Shared Memory): Per-SM, <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>192–228 KB/SM (A100: 164 KB configurable). Bandwidth: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>19 TB/s aggregate. Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>20 cycles.</li><li>L2 Cache: Shared across GPU, 40–60 MB (H100: 50 MB). Bandwidth: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5 TB/s. Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>200 cycles.</li><li>HBM: Main GPU memory, 80 GB (A100). Bandwidth: 2–3.35 TB/s. Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>400 cycles.</li><li>CPU DRAM: Via PCIe, 512 GB+. Bandwidth: 32–64 GB/s. Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>10K cycles.</li></ol><p>Why it matters for LLMs: Autoregressive generation reads the full model weights (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>140 GB for 70B) for every single token. At 2 TB/s HBM bandwidth, that’s 70ms just to stream the weights. The actual computation (one matrix-vector multiply) takes only 0.5ms. The GPU is 99% waiting for data.</p><p>Flash Attention exploits this: By keeping intermediate results (QK scores, softmax) in SRAM (19 TB/s) instead of writing them to HBM (2 TB/s), it eliminates 90% of the memory traffic for attention. The compute is the same, but HBM reads/writes drop 10<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 2 (Systems Foundations for LLMs).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q31">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q31: How does Flash Attention work? What is the online softmax trick?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Problem: Standard attention materializes the <math alttext="n\times n" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></semantics></math> attention matrix in HBM. For <math alttext="n=8192" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>8192</mn></mrow></semantics></math>: <math alttext="8192^{2}\times 2=134" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msup><mn>8192</mn><mn>2</mn></msup><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow><mo>=</mo><mn>134</mn></mrow></semantics></math> MB per head, 4.3 GB per layer with 32 heads. Must write to HBM then read back for softmax and multiply — 3 full HBM round-trips.</p><p>Flash Attention solution: Never store the full <math alttext="n\times n" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></semantics></math> matrix. Process in tiles that fit in SRAM.</p><p>Algorithm:</p><ol><li>Split <math alttext="Q" display="inline"><semantics><mi>Q</mi></semantics></math> into blocks of size <math alttext="B_{r}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>B</mi><mi>r</mi></msub></semantics></math> rows, <math alttext="K/V" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>K</mi><mo>/</mo><mi>V</mi></mrow></semantics></math> into blocks of <math alttext="B_{c}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>B</mi><mi>c</mi></msub></semantics></math> rows.</li><li>For each <math alttext="Q" display="inline"><semantics><mi>Q</mi></semantics></math> block: iterate over all <math alttext="K" display="inline"><semantics><mi>K</mi></semantics></math> blocks, computing partial attention scores.</li><li>Online softmax trick: Maintain running max <math alttext="m" display="inline"><semantics><mi>m</mi></semantics></math> and running sum <math alttext="\ell" display="inline"><semantics><mi mathvariant="normal">ℓ</mi></semantics></math> for softmax normalization. When processing a new <math alttext="K" display="inline"><semantics><mi>K</mi></semantics></math> block, update: <math alttext="m_{\text{new}}=\max(m_{\text{old}},\max(\text{scores}))" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>m</mi><mtext>new</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>max</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>m</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub><mo>,</mo><mrow><mi>max</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mtext>scores</mtext><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>, rescale previous accumulator by <math alttext="e^{m_{\text{old}}-m_{\text{new}}}" display="inline"><semantics><msup><mi>e</mi><mrow><msub><mi>m</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>m</mi><mtext>new</mtext></msub></mrow></msup></semantics></math>, add new contribution.</li><li>Output is accumulated incrementally — never needs the full <math alttext="n\times n" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></semantics></math> matrix.</li></ol><p>Key insight: Softmax is normally a global operation (<math alttext="\max" display="inline"><semantics><mi>max</mi></semantics></math> and <math alttext="\sum" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∑</mo></semantics></math> over all elements). The online trick decomposes it into local updates with a correction factor. Mathematically exact — no approximation.</p><p>Result: Memory <math alttext="O(n)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>n</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> instead of <math alttext="O(n^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Speed 2–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> faster (fewer HBM accesses, more time in SRAM).</p><p>Flash Attention 2: Better work partitioning across warps, reduces non-matmul FLOPs by 2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>.</p><p>Flash Attention 3 (H100/Hopper): Uses Tensor Memory Accelerator (TMA) for async loads, warp specialization (producer/consumer warps), FP8 support.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 2 (LLM Architecture; Systems Foundations).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q32">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q32: Explain PagedAttention. How does it solve the KV cache problem?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The problem: During generation, each sequence needs a KV cache (stores K and V tensors for all previous tokens). For a 70B model: each token needs <math alttext="2\times n_{\text{layers}}\times d_{\text{model}}\times 2" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><msub><mi>n</mi><mtext>layers</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><msub><mi>d</mi><mtext>model</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow></semantics></math> bytes = <math alttext="2\times 80\times 8192\times 2\approx 2.5" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>80</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>8192</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>2</mn></mrow><mo>≈</mo><mn>2.5</mn></mrow></semantics></math> MB per token. A 2048-token sequence: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5 GB of KV cache.</p><p>Traditional allocation: Pre-allocate max_sequence_length for each active sequence. If max=2048 but average=500, you waste 75% of allocated memory. With 50 concurrent sequences, that’s hundreds of GB wasted.</p><p>PagedAttention: Inspired by OS virtual memory:</p><ol><li>KV cache is split into fixed-size <em>blocks</em> (pages), each holding KV for 16 tokens.</li><li>A <em>block table</em> (like a page table) maps logical token positions to physical memory blocks.</li><li>Blocks are allocated on demand as the sequence grows. No pre-allocation waste.</li><li>Freed blocks return to a pool immediately when sequences finish.</li></ol><p>Extra benefits:</p><ul><li>Prefix sharing: Multiple sequences with the same system prompt share KV cache blocks (copy-on-write). Saves 30–50% memory for chat applications.</li><li>Preemption: Can “swap out” a low-priority sequence’s blocks to CPU, freeing GPU memory for higher-priority requests.</li><li>Near-zero fragmentation: Internal fragmentation limited to last block (<math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>16 tokens). External fragmentation eliminated (any free block can be used anywhere).</li></ul><p>Result: 3–5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> more concurrent sequences in the same memory <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> 3–5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> better throughput for serving.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 2 (Systems Foundations for LLMs).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q33: Compare NVLink vs InfiniBand. When do you use each in RLHF training?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>NVLink (intra-node, GPU-to-GPU):</p><ul><li>Bandwidth: 600 GB/s (A100), 900 GB/s (H100) — total bidirectional</li><li>Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>1 <math alttext="\mu" display="inline"><semantics><mi>μ</mi></semantics></math>s</li><li>Scope: Within one physical node (8 GPUs connected via NVSwitch)</li><li>Use case: Tensor Parallelism (TP=8). Each layer’s matrix multiply is split across GPUs, requiring AllReduce after every layer. Needs ultra-high bandwidth + low latency.</li></ul><p>InfiniBand NDR (inter-node, node-to-node):</p><ul><li>Bandwidth: 400 Gb/s = 50 GB/s per port. With 8 ports (GPUDirect RDMA): 400 GB/s aggregate per node.</li><li>Latency: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>1–5 <math alttext="\mu" display="inline"><semantics><mi>μ</mi></semantics></math>s (RDMA)</li><li>Scope: Between nodes in a cluster. Requires switches (fat-tree topology).</li><li>Use case: Data Parallelism / FSDP gradient synchronization. Multiple vLLM instances across nodes don’t communicate (embarrassingly parallel).</li><li><em>Training</em>: TP=8 over NVLink intra-node + FSDP over InfiniBand inter-node. Gradients synced after full backward pass.</li><li><em>Weight sync</em>: Training <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> Generation uses InfiniBand (140 GB transfer, async, takes <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>3s at 50 GB/s).</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapters 2 and 11 (Systems Foundations; System Architecture).</em></p>
</div>

## Optimization and Training Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q34">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q34: Explain Adam vs AdamW. Why does the difference matter for LLMs?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Adam with L2 regularization: <math alttext="\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}-\alpha\cdot(\hat{m}_{t}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon)+\lambda\theta_{t})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>θ</mi><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>θ</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>m</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msqrt><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>v</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>t</mi></msub></msqrt><mo>+</mo><mi>ϵ</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>λ</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>θ</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. The weight decay term <math alttext="\lambda\theta_{t}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>θ</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></mrow></semantics></math> is <em>inside</em> the adaptive scaling. Parameters with large gradients (large <math alttext="v_{t}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>v</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></semantics></math>) get <em>less</em> weight decay (divided by <math alttext="\sqrt{v_{t}}" display="inline"><semantics><msqrt><msub><mi>v</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></msqrt></semantics></math>). This is not true weight decay — it’s scale-dependent.</p><p>AdamW (decoupled weight decay): <math alttext="\theta_{t+1}=(1-\alpha\lambda)\theta_{t}-\alpha\cdot\hat{m}_{t}/(\sqrt{\hat{v}_{t}}+\epsilon)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>θ</mi><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo>+</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>λ</mi></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>θ</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>α</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>m</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>t</mi></msub></mrow><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msqrt><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>v</mi><mo>^</mo></mover><mi>t</mi></msub></msqrt><mo>+</mo><mi>ϵ</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Weight decay is applied <em>outside</em> and <em>before</em> the adaptive update. Every parameter gets the same proportional decay regardless of gradient history.</p><p>Why it matters for LLMs:</p><ol><li>LLMs have parameters spanning many orders of magnitude (embedding layers vs attention vs FFN). Adam’s coupled L2 effectively penalizes small-gradient params more than large-gradient ones — wrong behavior.</li><li>Decoupled WD provides uniform regularization across all layers, preventing some layers from growing unbounded while others over-shrink.</li><li>Empirically: AdamW gives 2–5% better perplexity on long pretraining runs compared to Adam+L2 with the same effective regularization.</li></ol><p>For RL specifically: Often use <math alttext="\lambda=0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math> (no weight decay). The KL penalty provides regularization. But for SFT: <math alttext="\lambda=0.01" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>λ</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0.01</mn></mrow></semantics></math>–<math alttext="0.1" display="inline"><semantics><mn>0.1</mn></semantics></math> with AdamW is standard.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q35">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q35: Why is learning rate warmup necessary? What happens without it?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The problem: Adam’s second moment estimate <math alttext="v_{t}=\beta_{2}v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_{2})g_{t}^{2}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>v</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>β</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>v</mi><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msub></mrow><mo>+</mo><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>β</mi><mn>2</mn></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msubsup><mi>g</mi><mi>t</mi><mn>2</mn></msubsup></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> starts at <math alttext="v_{0}=0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>v</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. By the time LR reaches full value, <math alttext="v_{t}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>v</mi><mi>t</mi></msub></semantics></math> has accumulated enough samples to be accurate.</p><p>Typical settings:</p><ul><li>Pretraining: 2000 steps warmup (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>1% of 200K steps)</li><li>SFT: 100 steps warmup (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5% of 2000 steps)</li><li>RL (PPO/GRPO): 20–50 steps warmup (short, model already stable from SFT)</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 10 (LLM Architecture; SFT Best Practices).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q36">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q36: Compare learning rate schedules. Which would you choose for RL fine-tuning?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Cosine decay: <math alttext="\eta_{t}=\eta_{\text{min}}+\frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\text{max}}-\eta_{\text{min}})(1+\cos(\pi t/T))" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mtext>min</mtext></msub><mo>+</mo><mrow><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mn>2</mn></mfrac><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mtext>max</mtext></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mi>η</mi><mtext>min</mtext></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>cos</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>π</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow><mo>/</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Standard for pretraining and SFT. Smooth decay, most time at moderate LR.</p><p>Linear decay: <math alttext="\eta_{t}=\eta_{\text{max}}(1-t/T)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mtext>max</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo>/</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Simpler, similar results to cosine for short runs.</p><p>WSD (Warmup-Stable-Decay): Warmup <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> constant LR for 80% <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> rapid decay in last 20%. New standard for pretraining. The “stable” phase gives consistent learning; the final decay squeezes out remaining gains.</p><p>Constant: No decay. <math alttext="\eta_{t}=\eta_{\text{max}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><msub><mi>η</mi><mtext>max</mtext></msub></mrow></semantics></math> after warmup.</p><p>For RL fine-tuning (PPO/GRPO), I’d choose: Constant with short warmup. Reasons:</p><ol><li>RL training length is highly unpredictable (you stop based on win-rate, not epochs).</li><li>Cosine/linear decay assumes you know the total steps in advance.</li><li>The LR is already very low (<math alttext="10^{-6}" display="inline"><semantics><msup><mn>10</mn><mrow><mo>−</mo><mn>6</mn></mrow></msup></semantics></math>), further decay makes updates invisible.</li><li>PPO’s adaptive KL controller already modulates the effective step size.</li><li>If you must decay: use linear decay over a generous budget, stop early when metrics plateau.</li></ol><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 5 (LLM Architecture; PPO).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q37">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q37: Why is gradient clipping critical for RL training but less important for SFT?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>SFT: Supervised loss is smooth and well-behaved. Gradient norms are consistent across batches (typically 0.1–1.0). Clipping at 1.0 rarely activates — it’s a safety net.</p><p>RL (PPO/GRPO): Gradient norms are highly variable because:</p><ol><li>Reward variance: One batch might have all high-reward responses, next might have all low. The advantage <math alttext="\hat{A}" display="inline"><semantics><mover accent="true"><mi>A</mi><mo>^</mo></mover></semantics></math> swings wildly.</li><li>Ratio explosion: If a rare token’s probability changed a lot, <math alttext="r_{t}=\pi_{\text{new}}/\pi_{\text{old}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>r</mi><mi>t</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>new</mtext></msub><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>π</mi><mtext>old</mtext></msub></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> can be very large <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> large gradient before clipping kicks in.</li><li>Sparse reward: In GRPO with binary rewards, some prompts give all-correct (advantage <math alttext="\approx 0" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>≈</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow></semantics></math>) then suddenly a hard prompt gives extreme advantages.</li><li>KL term: The KL penalty gradient can spike when policy diverges.</li></ol><p>Without clipping: A single bad batch can produce a gradient 100<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> normal magnitude <math alttext="\rightarrow" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> destroys the model in one step. Recovery is impossible (catastrophic forgetting of all pretraining).</p><p>Typical setting: <code>max_grad_norm=1.0</code>. Some use 0.5 for extra safety in early RL training. The norm is computed globally across all parameters (not per-layer).</p><p>Monitoring: If clipping activates more than 20% of steps, your LR is probably too high or your batch size too small.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 5 and 7 (PPO; GRPO).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q38">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q38: BF16 vs FP16 for training. When does the choice matter?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>FP16: 1 sign + 5 exponent + 10 mantissa bits. Range: <math alttext="\pm 65504" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo>±</mo><mn>65504</mn></mrow></semantics></math>. Precision: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>3.3 decimal digits.</p><p>BF16: 1 sign + 8 exponent + 7 mantissa bits. Range: <math alttext="\pm 3.4\times 10^{38}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo>±</mo><mn>3.4</mn><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mn>10</mn><msup><mrow></mrow><mn>38</mn></msup></mrow></semantics></math> (same as FP32!). Precision: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>2.4 decimal digits.</p><p>Why BF16 wins for LLMs:</p><ol><li>No loss scaling needed: FP16’s small range (<math alttext="\pm" display="inline"><semantics><mo>±</mo></semantics></math>65K) means gradients and activations frequently overflow/underflow. Requires dynamic loss scaling (multiply loss by 1024, divide gradients back). BF16 has FP32’s range — overflow is essentially impossible.</li><li>Simpler code: No loss scaler, no inf/nan checking, no dynamic scaling adjustment.</li><li>Critical for RL: RL gradients are noisier and spikier than SFT. FP16 loss scaling often fails (picks wrong scale, causes NaN). BF16 “just works.”</li></ol><p>When FP16 might be better: If you need maximum precision (some scientific computing tasks) and can manage the loss scaling. FP16 has 3 more mantissa bits = slightly more accurate results.</p><p>FP32 master weights: Even with BF16 forward/backward, accumulate gradient updates in FP32 to prevent rounding errors from compounding over thousands of small steps. Standard practice for all LLM training.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 2 (Systems Foundations for LLMs).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q39">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q39: Derive the Bradley-Terry reward model loss. What are its limitations?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Bradley-Terry model: Given two responses, the probability the better one (<math alttext="y_{w}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub></semantics></math>) is preferred: <math alttext="P(y_{w}\succ y_{l}|x)=\sigma(r(x,y_{w})-r(x,y_{l}))" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo>≻</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub></mrow><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">|</mo><mi>x</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>σ</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>−</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>x</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> where <math alttext="\sigma" display="inline"><semantics><mi>σ</mi></semantics></math> is the sigmoid.</p><p>MLE derivation: Given <math alttext="N" display="inline"><semantics><mi>N</mi></semantics></math> preference pairs, maximize likelihood: <math alttext="\prod_{i}P(y_{w}^{i}\succ y_{l}^{i})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mo>∏</mo><mi>i</mi></msub><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msubsup><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi><mi>i</mi></msubsup><mo>≻</mo><msubsup><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi><mi>i</mi></msubsup></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Take negative log: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i}\log\sigma(r(x_{i},y_{w}^{i})-r(x_{i},y_{l}^{i}))" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ℒ</mi><mo rspace="0em">=</mo><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0.055em">−</mo><msub><mo>∑</mo><mi>i</mi></msub><mi>log</mi><mi>σ</mi><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>r</mi><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msubsup><mi>y</mi><mi>w</mi><mi>i</mi></msubsup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo>−</mo><mi>r</mi><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>x</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msubsup><mi>y</mi><mi>l</mi><mi>i</mi></msubsup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>Limitations:</p><ol><li>No ties: BT can’t model “equally good” — forces a strict preference.</li><li>Transitivity: Assumes if A<math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>B and B<math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>C then A<math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>C. Humans aren’t transitive.</li><li>Context-free: Same reward regardless of what alternatives were available.</li><li>Scalar collapse: Compresses all quality dimensions into one number. A response can be safe but unhelpful — RM must trade off.</li><li>Length bias: Longer responses get higher scores (more information = more likely to contain what annotator wanted). Must explicitly decorrelate.</li></ol><p>Mitigations: Margin loss (require minimum gap <math alttext="\delta" display="inline"><semantics><mi>δ</mi></semantics></math>), reward centering (subtract running mean), length penalty during training, multi-head RM (separate scores for helpfulness/safety/accuracy).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 9 (Reward Model Training).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q40">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q40: What is sequence packing in SFT and why does it matter?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Problem: Training examples have variable length. Standard batching pads all examples to max_length. If max=4096 but average=500, you waste 88% of compute on padding tokens (which contribute zero gradient).</p><p>Packing solution: Concatenate multiple short examples into a single max_length sequence. Separate with EOS tokens. Train on all examples simultaneously.</p><p>Example: Instead of 4 sequences padded to 4096 (16K tokens, 14K padding), pack into 1 sequence of 4096 with 4 examples end-to-end (4096 real tokens, 0 padding). 4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> more efficient.</p><p>Critical detail — block-diagonal attention mask: Without special handling, example 2 attends to example 1’s tokens (cross-contamination). Must use a block-diagonal attention mask that restricts each example to only attend to its own tokens.</p><p>In TRL: <code>SFTConfig(packing=True, max_seq_length=4096)</code>. Handles mask automatically.</p><p>Caveats: (1) Longer examples still need their own batch entries (can’t split mid-sequence). (2) Slight implementation complexity for position embeddings (reset per example). (3) Some argue packing changes the effective batch size (more examples per step) — adjust LR accordingly.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 10 (SFT Best Practices and Techniques).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q41">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q41: Explain completion-only masking for SFT. What happens if you don’t use it?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>In chat-format SFT data: <code>[system] + [user message] + [assistant response]</code>. Standard NLL loss computes loss on all tokens including the system prompt and user message.</p><p>Problem without masking: The model wastes capacity learning to predict the user’s message (which it will never need to generate at inference). Worse: if the training data has diverse user messages, the model gets confused about “whose turn is it?”</p><p>Completion-only masking: Set loss weight to 0 for all tokens in the prompt (system + user). Only compute loss on assistant response tokens.</p><p>TRL: <code>DataCollatorForCompletionOnlyLM(response_template="&lt;|assistant|&gt;")</code></p><p>Impact: Typically 5–15% better on instruction-following benchmarks. Faster convergence (gradient signal is concentrated on useful tokens). No change to compute cost.</p><p>Subtlety: Must include the response template token in the loss (teaches the model to start responding). But exclude everything before it.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 10 (SFT Best Practices and Techniques).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q42">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q42: How does SFT quality affect the RL ceiling? What is the pass@k diagnostic?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Ceiling theorem (informal): RL can only reinforce behaviors the model can already produce with non-negligible probability. If the SFT model has 0% chance of generating a correct solution, RL will never find it.</p><p>Why: GRPO/PPO sample from the current policy and reinforce good samples. If good samples don’t exist in the distribution, there’s nothing to reinforce. RL is exploration-limited by the base policy’s support.</p><p>pass@k diagnostic: Generate <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math> responses per prompt, check if <em>any</em> is correct:</p><ul><li>pass@1: Model’s typical performance (greedy/low temp).</li><li>pass@8: Upper bound of what GRPO with <math alttext="G=8" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>8</mn></mrow></semantics></math> can achieve.</li><li>pass@64: Upper bound for aggressive Best-of-N.</li><li>pass@256: Approximate ceiling for RL improvement.</li></ul><p>Interpretation:</p><ul><li>pass@1=20%, pass@64=80%: Great! 4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> headroom for RL. Strong gains expected.</li><li>pass@1=20%, pass@64=25%: Almost no headroom. RL won’t help much. Need better SFT first.</li><li>pass@1=5%, pass@64=60%: Model <em>can</em> solve it but rarely does. Perfect case for RL (reinforce the rare successes).</li></ul><p>Rule: If pass@64 <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math> 1.5<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> pass@1, invest in better SFT data before starting RL.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 10 (GRPO; SFT Best Practices).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q43">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q43: Design a multi-objective reward system for a chat model. How do you balance helpfulness vs safety?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Architecture: Separate reward models for each objective:</p><ul><li><math alttext="r_{\text{helpful}}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>helpful</mtext></msub></semantics></math>: Trained on helpfulness preferences (quality, accuracy, completeness)</li><li><math alttext="r_{\text{safe}}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>safe</mtext></msub></semantics></math>: Trained on safety preferences (refusals, harmlessness, no hallucination)</li><li><math alttext="r_{\text{format}}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>format</mtext></msub></semantics></math>: Rule-based (follows instructions, proper formatting, appropriate length)</li></ul><p>Combination strategies:</p><ol><li>Weighted sum (simplest): <math alttext="r=w_{1}r_{\text{helpful}}+w_{2}r_{\text{safe}}+w_{3}r_{\text{format}}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>w</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>helpful</mtext></msub></mrow><mo>+</mo><mrow><msub><mi>w</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>safe</mtext></msub></mrow><mo>+</mo><mrow><msub><mi>w</mi><mn>3</mn></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><msub><mi>r</mi><mtext>format</mtext></msub></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. Safety gets 2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> weight because its failure mode (harmful content) is much worse than helpfulness failure (mediocre answer).</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 9 and 12 (Reward Model Training; LLM Agentic Training).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q44">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q44: How does speculative decoding work? When does it help for RLHF?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Problem: Large model generates one token per forward pass (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>70ms for 70B). Slow.</p><p>Speculative decoding:</p><ol><li>Draft: Small model (1–7B) generates <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math> candidate tokens quickly (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5ms for all <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math>).</li><li>Verify: Large model does one forward pass scoring all <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math> tokens in parallel. Accepts tokens where <math alttext="p_{\text{large}}(t_{i})\geq p_{\text{draft}}(t_{i})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mrow><msub><mi>p</mi><mtext>large</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>t</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo>≥</mo><mrow><msub><mi>p</mi><mtext>draft</mtext></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>t</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> (always). Probabilistically accepts others.</li><li>Result: On average, 3–4 tokens accepted per verification step. Speedup: 2–3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>.</li></ol><p>Key property: The output distribution is <em>identical</em> to sampling from the large model alone. No quality loss. The draft model only affects speed, not output.</p><p>For RLHF specifically: Generation is 60% of compute. 2–3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> speedup on generation = 1.5–2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> end-to-end speedup. Combined with vLLM + INT8: generation goes from the bottleneck to parity with training.</p><p>Limitations: (1) Draft model must share tokenizer. (2) Less effective at high temperature (draft model less accurate). (3) Needs additional GPU memory for draft model. (4) Diminishing returns beyond <math alttext="k=5" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>k</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>5</mn></mrow></semantics></math> (acceptance rate drops).</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 2 and 11 (Systems Foundations; System Architecture).</em></p>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q45">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q45: Explain the roofline model. How do you determine if a kernel is compute-bound or memory-bound?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The roofline model plots achievable performance (FLOPS) as a function of arithmetic intensity (FLOPS per byte of memory traffic).</p><p>Two regimes:</p><ul><li>Memory-bound (left of crossover): Performance limited by how fast you can feed data to compute units. Actual FLOPS = bandwidth <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> arithmetic intensity. GPU utilization <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math> 100%.</li><li>Compute-bound (right of crossover): Performance limited by peak FLOPS. Memory is fast enough. GPU at max utilization.</li></ul><p>Crossover point: Peak FLOPS / Peak Bandwidth. For A100: 312 TF / 2 TB/s = 156 FLOP/byte.</p><p>LLM operations:</p><ul><li>Autoregressive generation (batch=1): Read 140GB weights, do 140G FLOPs = 1 FLOP/byte. <em>Extremely</em> memory-bound (156<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> below crossover). Only 0.6% GPU utilization.</li><li>Training forward pass (batch=128, seq=2048): Arithmetic intensity <math alttext="\approx 200" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mphantom></mphantom><mo>≈</mo><mn>200</mn></mrow></semantics></math>+ FLOP/byte. Compute-bound. Near peak utilization.</li><li>Attention (long sequence): <math alttext="O(n^{2}d)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>d</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> FLOPs / <math alttext="O(n^{2}+nd)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>d</mi></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> bytes. For long <math alttext="n" display="inline"><semantics><mi>n</mi></semantics></math>: compute-bound. For short <math alttext="n" display="inline"><semantics><mi>n</mi></semantics></math>: memory-bound. Flash Attention keeps it in SRAM regardless.</li></ul><p>Practical use: If your kernel is memory-bound, reduce memory traffic (quantization, caching, tiling). If compute-bound, reduce FLOPs (pruning, distillation, lower precision).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 2 (Systems Foundations for LLMs).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="q46">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q46: How does continuous batching work and why is it essential for RLHF generation?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Static batching: Start <math alttext="B" display="inline"><semantics><mi>B</mi></semantics></math> sequences. Wait for ALL to finish. If one sequence generates 500 tokens and another generates 50 tokens, the 50-token sequence’s GPU slot sits idle for 450 tokens.</p><p>Continuous batching (iteration-level scheduling): After each generation step, check which sequences are done. Immediately insert new sequences into freed slots. GPU slots are never idle.</p><p>Why essential for RLHF:</p><ol><li>RLHF generates diverse outputs (high temperature). Length variance is huge — some responses are 50 tokens, others 2000+.</li><li>Without continuous batching: average utilization <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>40–50% (waiting for slowest sequence).</li><li>With continuous batching: utilization <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>90%. Throughput 2–3<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> higher.</li><li>RLHF needs large batches (128+ responses per step). Generating 128 responses with static batching requires max_tokens <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> 128 sequential steps. Continuous batching amortizes this.</li></ol><p>Implementation: vLLM’s scheduler checks after every decode step. Preemption: if a new high-priority request arrives and memory is full, can swap out a low-priority sequence’s KV cache to CPU and resume later.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 2 and 11 (Systems Foundations; System Architecture).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

## Transformer Architecture Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s11-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why does RoPE dominate over learned absolute positional embeddings in modern LLMs?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>RoPE encodes <em>relative</em> position directly into the Q/K dot product via rotation matrices. Key advantages:</p><ol><li>Attention scores depend only on relative distance <math alttext="i-j" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>−</mo><mi>j</mi></mrow></semantics></math>, not absolute position — this generalizes better to unseen sequence lengths.</li><li>Can be extended beyond training length via frequency scaling (NTK-aware, YaRN) without retraining.</li><li>No additional parameters (rotations are deterministic from position index).</li><li>Learned absolute embeddings are fixed to training length and don’t extrapolate — a model trained at 4K context fails at 8K.</li></ol><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s11-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is Grouped Query Attention (GQA) and why does Llama-3 use it?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard MHA: <math alttext="H" display="inline"><semantics><mi>H</mi></semantics></math> query heads, <math alttext="H" display="inline"><semantics><mi>H</mi></semantics></math> key heads, <math alttext="H" display="inline"><semantics><mi>H</mi></semantics></math> value heads. GQA: <math alttext="H" display="inline"><semantics><mi>H</mi></semantics></math> query heads but only <math alttext="G&lt;H" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>G</mi><mo>&lt;</mo><mi>H</mi></mrow></semantics></math> key/value heads (shared across query groups).</p><p>Llama-3 70B: 64 query heads, 8 KV heads (each KV head shared by 8 query heads).</p><p>Benefits:</p><ul><li>KV cache size reduced by <math alttext="H/G=8\times" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>H</mi><mo>/</mo><mi>G</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>8</mn><mo lspace="0.222em">×</mo></mrow></semantics></math> — critical for inference (KV cache is the dominant memory cost at long sequences).</li><li>Minimal quality loss (<math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>0.5% on benchmarks) because KV patterns are highly correlated across heads.</li><li>Inference throughput increases proportionally to KV cache reduction (more sequences fit in memory = higher batch size).</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s11-p4">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why did decoder-only architectures win over encoder-decoder for LLMs?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<ol><li>Unified objective: Pretraining = fine-tuning = inference all use next-token prediction. No architectural mismatch.</li><li>Parameter efficiency: All parameters contribute to generation. In encoder-decoder, encoder params are “wasted” during pure generation tasks.</li><li>Simpler scaling: One model, one loss function, one set of hyperparameters to tune.</li><li>KV cache efficiency: Decoder-only has one KV cache; encoder-decoder has two (encoder + decoder cross-attention).</li><li>Emergent few-shot: Decoder-only naturally supports in-context learning (prepend examples to the prompt).</li></ol><p>Encoder-decoder still wins for seq2seq tasks with fixed input length (translation), but these are a shrinking fraction of LLM use cases.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

## Flash Attention Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s12-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Flash Attention computes the same result as standard attention but is 2–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> faster. How is this possible if it does the same number of FLOPs?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Flash Attention is faster because it reduces <em>HBM memory traffic</em>, not FLOPs. Standard attention materializes the <math alttext="n\times n" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></semantics></math> attention matrix in HBM (slow), reads it back for softmax, reads again for <math alttext="PV" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>P</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>V</mi></mrow></semantics></math> multiply — 4 HBM round-trips over <math alttext="O(n^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> data.</p><p>Flash Attention tiles the computation so the <math alttext="n\times n" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></semantics></math> matrix is computed and consumed entirely in SRAM (fast, 19 TB/s) without ever writing it to HBM (2 TB/s). The “online softmax” trick enables this by maintaining running statistics.</p><p>Result: HBM traffic drops from <math alttext="O(n^{2}d)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>d</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> to <math alttext="O(n^{2}d/M)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>d</mi></mrow><mo>/</mo><mi>M</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> where <math alttext="M" display="inline"><semantics><mi>M</mi></semantics></math> is SRAM size. Same FLOPs, 10–50<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> less memory traffic <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> 2–4<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> wall-clock speedup.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 2 (LLM Architecture; Systems Foundations).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s12-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why doesn’t Flash Attention help the FFN layers?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>FFN layers are <em>compute-bound</em>, not memory-bound. Their arithmetic intensity (<math alttext="I\approx 300" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo>≈</mo><mn>300</mn></mrow></semantics></math> FLOP/byte for large batch GEMMs) is already above the roofline ridge point (156 FLOP/byte on A100).</p><p>Flash Attention helps attention because attention is deeply memory-bound (<math alttext="I\approx 1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo>≈</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math>–<math alttext="60" display="inline"><semantics><mn>60</mn></semantics></math> FLOP/byte). By keeping data in SRAM, it removes the memory bottleneck.</p><p>For FFN: the bottleneck is already the Tensor Cores (not memory bandwidth), so reducing memory traffic doesn’t help. Instead, FFN benefits from quantization (reduces weight size <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> higher arithmetic intensity) and larger batch sizes.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 2 (LLM Architecture; Systems Foundations).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s12-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain the online softmax trick and why it’s essential for Flash Attention.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard softmax needs the global maximum <math alttext="m=\max_{j}x_{j}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>m</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>max</mi><mi>j</mi></msub><mo ## LoRA and PEFT Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s13-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why does LoRA work? What theoretical insight justifies low-rank updates?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Aghajanyan et al. [3] showed that fine-tuning operates in a very low <em>intrinsic dimensionality</em> — the effective parameter space for a fine-tuning task is far smaller than the model’s total parameter count. A 175B model may have intrinsic dimensionality <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>10,000 for a given task.</p><p>LoRA directly exploits this: by constraining updates to rank <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math> (<math alttext="W^{\prime}=W+BA" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msup><mi>W</mi><mo>′</mo></msup><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>W</mi><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>B</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>A</mi></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>, <math alttext="B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>B</mi><mo>∈</mo><msup><mi>ℝ</mi><mrow><mi>d</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">×</mo><mi>r</mi></mrow></msup></mrow></semantics></math>), it restricts learning to an <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math>-dimensional subspace per weight matrix. Since the true task subspace is low-dimensional, this loses almost nothing while reducing trainable parameters by 100–1000<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math>.</p><p>Intuition: Fine-tuning doesn’t change what the model “knows” (the full-rank <math alttext="W" display="inline"><semantics><mi>W</mi></semantics></math> stays frozen); it only adjusts <em>how</em> existing knowledge is combined for the new task — a low-rank perturbation.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s13-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Compare QLoRA vs. full LoRA vs. full fine-tuning for a 70B model. When would you choose each?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Decision tree: (1) If task requires deep knowledge change <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> full fine-tune. (2) If adapting to new style/format <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> LoRA. (3) If memory-constrained or rapid iteration <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> QLoRA. (4) If rank matters: start <math alttext="r=16" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>16</mn></mrow></semantics></math>; increase if training loss plateaus above full fine-tune level.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 10 (LLM Architecture; SFT Best Practices).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s13-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is DoRA and why does it outperform standard LoRA?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>DoRA (Weight-Decomposed Low-Rank Adaptation) decomposes <math alttext="W" display="inline"><semantics><mi>W</mi></semantics></math> into magnitude <math alttext="\|W\|" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo><mi>W</mi><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo></mrow></semantics></math> and direction <math alttext="W/\|W\|" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>W</mi><mo>/</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo><mi>W</mi><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>, then applies LoRA only to the direction component:</p><div class="hh-equation"><math alttext="W^{\prime}=m\odot\frac{W+BA}{\|W+BA\|}" display="block"><semantics><mrow><msup><mi>W</mi><mo>′</mo></msup><mo>=</mo><mrow><mi>m</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⊙</mo><mfrac><mrow><mi>W</mi><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>B</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>A</mi></mrow></mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo><mrow><mi>W</mi><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>B</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>A</mi></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mrow></semantics></math></div><p>where <math alttext="m" display="inline"><semantics><mi>m</mi></semantics></math> (magnitude) is also trainable but as a simple scalar per output neuron.</p><p>Why it helps: Full fine-tuning naturally updates both magnitude and direction independently. Standard LoRA couples them (the low-rank update changes both simultaneously in a constrained way). DoRA decouples them, giving LoRA the same “degrees of freedom” structure as full fine-tuning. Result: 1–3% improvement on reasoning tasks with no extra compute at inference (merge adapters).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

## Model Compression Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s14-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain AWQ. Why does protecting 1% of weights preserve 99% of quality?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>AWQ (Activation-Aware Weight Quantization) observes that weight importance is highly non-uniform: weights that multiply large activations contribute disproportionately to the output.</p><p>The key insight: <math alttext="\|W\cdot X\|" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo><mrow><mi>W</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mi>X</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">‖</mo></mrow></semantics></math> depends on both <math alttext="W" display="inline"><semantics><mi>W</mi></semantics></math> and <math alttext="X" display="inline"><semantics><mi>X</mi></semantics></math>. A small weight multiplied by a large activation matters more than a large weight multiplied by a near-zero activation.</p><p>AWQ identifies the top 1% of “salient” channels (those with consistently large activation magnitudes across calibration data) and protects them by scaling: multiply salient channels by a factor <math alttext="s&gt;1" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>s</mi><mo>&gt;</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></semantics></math> before quantization (then divide by <math alttext="s" display="inline"><semantics><mi>s</mi></semantics></math> in the activation). This reduces relative quantization error for important channels.</p><p>Result: 4-bit quantization with <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>1% quality loss on 70B models, because the 99% of non-salient weights can tolerate aggressive quantization.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 1 and 2 (LLM Architecture; Systems Foundations).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s14-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: When should you use FP8 vs. 4-bit quantization vs. BF16?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<ul><li>BF16: Training (policy model in RLHF), when precision matters. Default for any model being updated by gradients.</li><li>FP8 (E4M3): H100 training with Transformer Engine (2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> throughput, <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>0.5% quality loss). Also for inference on H100 when you need maximum throughput.</li><li>INT8/FP8 inference: Frozen models in RLHF (reference model, reward model) — not being trained, so reduced precision is safe.</li><li>4-bit (AWQ/GPTQ): Inference serving at scale. </div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s14-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain NVIDIA 2:4 structured sparsity. What’s the speedup and constraint?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>2:4 sparsity means: in every group of 4 consecutive elements, exactly 2 must be zero. This is enforced at the weight level.</p><p>Hardware support: A100/H100 Tensor Cores have dedicated 2:4 sparse GEMM instructions that skip the zero elements, achieving exactly 2<math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> throughput with no software overhead.</p><p>Constraint: You must achieve <em>exactly</em> 50% sparsity in this specific pattern. You can’t have 30% or 70% sparsity; you can’t have arbitrary sparsity patterns. The pruning must respect the 4-element group structure.</p><p>How to achieve it: After training (or during fine-tuning), for each group of 4 weights, zero out the 2 smallest by magnitude. Then fine-tune for a few hundred steps to recover quality. Quality loss: typically <math alttext="&lt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&lt;</mo></semantics></math>1% for large models (70B+).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 2 (Systems Foundations for LLMs).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s15-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Mixtral 8x7B has 47B total parameters but only 13B active per token. Explain how this works and why it’s efficient.</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Mixtral replaces each FFN layer with 8 parallel expert FFNs (each <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>7B params for the FFN portion). FFN experts: 8 <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math><math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5.25B = 42B. Total: <math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>47B.</p><p>Why 13B active: Per token, only 2 experts fire. Active params = attention (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>5B) + 2 FFN experts (<math alttext="\sim" display="inline"><semantics><mo>∼</mo></semantics></math>2 <math alttext="\times" display="inline"><semantics><mo>×</mo></semantics></math> 5.25B) <math alttext="\approx" display="inline"><semantics><mo>≈</mo></semantics></math> 13B.</p><p>Why efficient: Compute cost scales with <em>active</em> params (13B), matching a 13B dense model. But capacity (knowledge stored) scales with <em>total</em> params (47B), matching much larger models. </div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s15-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is the load balancing problem in MoE and how is it solved?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Without constraints, the router tends to send most tokens to 1–2 “popular” experts (rich-get-richer dynamics). This causes:</p><ul><li>Capacity waste: 6 of 8 experts are unused, model effectively shrinks to 2-expert size.</li><li>Compute imbalance: If each expert is on a different GPU, popular experts become bottlenecks while others idle.</li></ul><p>Solution: Auxiliary load-balancing loss: <math alttext="\mathcal{L}_{\text{bal}}=\alpha\cdot N\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}\cdot p_{i}" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>ℒ</mi><msub><mrow></mrow><mtext>bal</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mi>α</mi><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mi>N</mi><mo>∑</mo><msub><mrow></mrow><mrow><mi>i</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow></msub><msup><mrow></mrow><mi>N</mi></msup><mi>f</mi><msub><mrow></mrow><mi>i</mi></msub><mo lspace="0.222em" rspace="0.222em">⋅</mo><mi>p</mi><msub><mrow></mrow><mi>i</mi></msub></mrow></semantics></math>, where <math alttext="f_{i}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>f</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></semantics></math> = fraction of tokens routed to expert <math alttext="i" display="inline"><semantics><mi>i</mi></semantics></math>, <math alttext="p_{i}" display="inline"><semantics><msub><mi>p</mi><mi>i</mi></msub></semantics></math> = mean router probability for expert <math alttext="i" display="inline"><semantics><mi>i</mi></semantics></math>. This penalizes uneven distributions.</p><p>Alternative: Expert capacity factor — hard cap on max tokens per expert per batch. Overflow tokens are dropped or re-routed.</p><p>Typical <math alttext="\alpha" display="inline"><semantics><mi>α</mi></semantics></math>: 0.01–0.1 (small enough not to hurt main loss, large enough to prevent collapse).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 1 (LLM Architecture and Optimization Methods).</em></p>
</div>

## Agentic RL Questions

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s18-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why does standard RLHF (single-turn PPO/DPO) fail for multi-step agents?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard RLHF optimizes for single-turn quality: given a prompt, produce one good response. Multi-step agents face fundamentally different challenges:</p><ol><li>Credit assignment: In a 50-step trajectory, which step caused the failure? Single-turn reward assigns credit to the entire response uniformly; multi-step needs <em>per-step</em> credit.</li><li>Sparse rewards: Success/failure only at trajectory end. PPO’s GAE assumes intermediate rewards; without them, advantage estimates are noisy.</li><li>Action space: Actions are structured tool calls (JSON), not just token sequences. The model must learn syntax + semantics + strategy simultaneously.</li><li>Non-stationarity: The environment changes with each action (tool outputs modify state). Each step has a different “prompt” unlike single-turn where input is fixed.</li><li>Exploration: Agent must discover novel tool-use strategies, not just rephrase text.</li></ol><p>Solution: Trajectory-level GRPO (rank complete trajectories), process reward models (per-step feedback), or filtered SFT on successful trajectories.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 12 (LLM Agentic Training).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s18-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain how GRPO is adapted for agentic training. What are the key differences from single-turn GRPO?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Single-turn GRPO: generate <math alttext="N" display="inline"><semantics><mi>N</mi></semantics></math> responses to a prompt, rank by reward, compute advantages.</p><p>Agentic GRPO differences:</p><ol><li>Unit of generation: A full <em>trajectory</em> (10–100 steps) instead of a single response. Each trajectory is one “sample” in the group.</li><li>Reward: Terminal (task success/failure) or trajectory-level (sum of step rewards). NOT per-token.</li><li>Masking: Only compute policy loss on the agent’s outputs (reasoning + tool calls). Mask tool <em>outputs</em> (environment responses) from gradient computation.</li><li>Group size: Typically smaller (<math alttext="N=4" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>N</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>4</mn></mrow></semantics></math>–8) because trajectories are expensive (many forward passes per trajectory).</li><li>KL penalty: Applied per-step to prevent drift from SFT policy at each decision point.</li><li>Length normalization: Normalize by number of agent <em>actions</em> (not tokens) to avoid penalizing thorough reasoning.</li></ol><p><em>Review: Chapters 7 and 12 (GRPO; LLM Agentic Training).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Why does DeepSeek-R1 not use a Process Reward Model despite training on long reasoning chains?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>DeepSeek-R1 uses only outcome-based rewards (accuracy + format) for several reasons:</p><ol><li>Verifiable tasks: Math and code have deterministic ground-truth answers. <details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s21-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: How do you detect and mitigate benchmark contamination?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Contamination: Training data contains benchmark test examples (or close paraphrases), inflating scores.</p><p>Detection methods:</p><ol><li>N-gram overlap: Check if training data contains exact or near-exact matches to test items. 8-gram overlap with <math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>80% coverage is suspicious.</li><li>Canary strings: Insert unique identifiers in test sets; check if model can reproduce them.</li><li>Rephrased benchmarks: Create semantically equivalent but textually different versions of benchmarks. Large accuracy drops suggest memorization.</li><li>Temporal analysis: Model performance on pre-training-cutoff vs post-cutoff test items. Unusually high performance on old items suggests contamination.</li><li>Membership inference: Statistical tests for whether specific examples were in training data.</li></ol><p>Mitigation:</p><ul><li>Dynamic benchmarks: Regularly generate new test items (LiveCodeBench, Chatbot Arena)</li><li>Private test sets: Keep test items secret (LMSYS)</li><li>Decontamination during training: Remove detected overlaps from training data</li><li>Report contamination analysis: Disclose overlap metrics alongside benchmark scores</li></ul><p><em>Review: Chapter 14 (LLM Evaluation).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s21-p4">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain position bias in LLM-as-Judge and how to mitigate it</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Position bias: When using an LLM to judge two responses (A vs B), the model systematically prefers the response in a particular position (usually first or last), regardless of quality.</p><p>Empirical magnitude: GPT-4 shows 10–15% position bias; Claude shows 5–10%. Smaller models show larger bias.</p><p>Mitigation strategies:</p><ol><li>Position swapping: Judge each pair twice (A-B and B-A). Final decision = majority. If disagreement, mark as “tie.” This eliminates systematic position bias but doubles cost.</li><li>Multi-judge panels: Use 3+ different models as judges. Majority vote reduces individual model biases.</li><li>Reference-guided: Provide a rubric or reference answer. Judges score each response independently against the rubric, then compare scores (eliminates pairwise comparison entirely).</li><li>Calibrated prompting: Add explicit instruction: “The order of presentation is random and should not influence your judgment.”</li></ol><p>Additional biases: Verbosity bias (prefers longer responses), self-enhancement bias (models prefer their own outputs), authority bias (defers to responses that cite sources).</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 14 (LLM Evaluation).</em></p>
</div>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s22-p1">

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: How can RL be used to train memory operations?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Memory operations (write/read/update/delete) can be actions in the agent’s MDP:</p><p>Formulation:</p><ul><li>State: Current context + memory state</li><li>Actions: Standard actions + <code>memory_write(key/value)</code>, <code>memory_read(query)</code>, <code>memory_delete(key)</code></li><li>Reward: Task success (did memory help?) + memory efficiency penalty (fewer reads = better)</li></ul><p>What RL learns:</p><ol><li>What to store: Important information (API keys/user preferences) vs ephemeral details</li><li>When to retrieve: Before answering domain questions vs during general chat</li><li>Compression policy: When to summarize old memories vs keep verbatim</li><li>Forgetting: When old information is stale and should be removed</li></ol><p>Training signal: Counterfactual — “would the agent have succeeded if it hadn’t stored/retrieved this memory?” Implemented via trajectory comparison: trajectories with good memory use get higher rewards.</p><p>Challenge: Delayed reward — storing information now may only help 100 steps later. <details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s23-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Compare ReAct vs Plan-and-Execute orchestration patterns</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>ReAct (Reason + Act):</p><ul><li>Loop: Thought <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> Action <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> Observation <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> Thought <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> …</li><li>Each step decides the next action based on all previous observations</li><li>Pro: Adaptive — can change direction based on tool outputs</li><li>Con: Myopic — no upfront planning; can get stuck in loops; each LLM call sees entire history (expensive)</li></ul><p>Plan-and-Execute:</p><ul><li>Phase 1: Generate full plan (list of steps)</li><li>Phase 2: Execute steps sequentially (simpler executor; possibly cheaper model)</li><li>Phase 3: Re-plan if execution fails</li><li>Pro: Efficient (planning once is cheaper than reasoning every step); parallelizable independent steps</li><li>Con: Brittle plans — if early steps fail the plan may be invalid. </div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s23-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: How do you detect and prevent infinite loops in agent execution?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Agents can enter infinite loops when they repeat the same action expecting different results.</p><p>Detection methods:</p><ol><li>Max iteration guard: Hard limit (e.g., 25 steps). Simple but loses work on genuinely long tasks.</li><li>Action hash window: Hash the last <math alttext="k" display="inline"><semantics><mi>k</mi></semantics></math> (action/observation) pairs. If current hash matches a hash from the last <math alttext="w" display="inline"><semantics><mi>w</mi></semantics></math> steps then loop detected.</li><li>Semantic similarity: Embed recent actions. If cosine similarity between consecutive actions exceeds threshold (<math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>0.95) then likely stuck.</li><li>Progress monitoring: Define task-specific progress metrics. If no progress in <math alttext="N" display="inline"><semantics><mi>N</mi></semantics></math> steps then intervene.</li></ol><p>Recovery strategies:</p><ol><li>Inject hint: Add system message: “You seem to be repeating actions. Try a different approach.”</li><li>Force different action: Mask the repeated action from the action space for the next step.</li><li>Escalate: Return to user with partial results and ask for guidance.</li><li>Backtrack: Reset to a checkpoint before the loop began and try alternative path.</li></ol><p>Best practice: Combine max iterations (safety net) + hash-based detection (early intervention) + graceful escalation (preserve user trust).</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 17 and 18 (Agent Harness; Agent Design Patterns).</em></p>
</div>

<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The Contract Net Protocol (CNP) is a task allocation mechanism from distributed AI:</p><p>Steps:</p><ol><li>Announce: Manager broadcasts task description to all available agents</li><li>Bid: Agents assess their capability and submit bids (confidence; estimated cost; estimated time)</li><li>Award: Manager selects best bid(s) based on criteria (capability/cost/availability)</li><li>Execute: Winning agent(s) perform the task</li><li>Report: Agent reports results back to manager</li></ol><p>For LLM agents:</p><ul><li>Bidding = self-assessment: Each agent LLM evaluates “can I do this task well?” and provides a confidence score. <div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Centralized (Supervisor):</p><ul><li>One orchestrator LLM routes tasks to specialist workers</li><li>Clear control flow; easy to debug (inspect supervisor decisions)</li><li>Single point of failure; supervisor becomes token bottleneck</li><li>Best for: well-defined workflows; small agent teams (3–5 agents)</li></ul><p>Decentralized (Peer-to-Peer):</p><ul><li>Agents communicate directly; no central coordinator</li><li>Resilient (no single point of failure); scales horizontally</li><li>Hard to debug (emergent behavior); potential for conflicts and deadlocks</li><li>Communication scales <math alttext="O(n^{2})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msup><mi>n</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> without structure</li><li>Best for: resilient systems; large agent populations; creative tasks where emergent behavior is desired</li></ul><p>Hybrid (Hierarchical): Tree structure with sub-managers. Combines benefits: local autonomy within groups and global coordination at the top. Communication scales <math alttext="O(n\log n)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>O</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mi>n</mi><mo lspace="0.167em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mi>log</mi><mo lspace="0.167em">⁡</mo><mi>n</mi></mrow></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>.</p><p>Decision framework: Use centralized if you need predictability and auditability. Use decentralized if you need resilience and creativity. Use hierarchical for large (<math alttext="&gt;" display="inline"><semantics><mo>&gt;</mo></semantics></math>10 agent) systems.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 23 (Multi-Agent Systems).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s26-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is CTDE and why is it important for training multi-agent LLM systems?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>CTDE = Centralized Training; Decentralized Execution.</p><p>The problem: In multi-agent RL each agent’s environment is non-stationary (other agents are changing their policies simultaneously). This makes independent training unstable.</p><p>CTDE solution:</p><ul><li>During training: A centralized critic has access to all agents’ observations and actions: <math alttext="V(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>V</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>s</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>s</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">…</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>s</mi><mi>n</mi></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>a</mi><mn>1</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>a</mi><mn>2</mn></msub><mo>,</mo><mi mathvariant="normal">…</mi><mo>,</mo><msub><mi>a</mi><mi>n</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. This stabilizes training by removing non-stationarity from the value function.</li><li>During execution: Each agent acts based only on its own observation: <math alttext="a_{i}=\pi_{i}(o_{i})" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>a</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>π</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><msub><mi>o</mi><mi>i</mi></msub><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></semantics></math>. No communication overhead at inference time.</li></ul><p>For LLM agents: The centralized critic can be a reward model that evaluates the <em>joint</em> output of all agents (e.g., did the team of agents produce a correct software system?) while each agent is trained to maximize its contribution to the team reward using counterfactual credit assignment.</p><p>Practical challenge: Full CTDE requires all agents to train simultaneously with shared state — expensive for LLMs. Approximations: train agents in rounds (freeze others and train one) or use population-based training with periodic synchronization.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 23 (Multi-Agent Systems).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s27-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: How do you test and evaluate an agent system in production?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Agent testing follows a testing pyramid:</p><p>Level 1 — Unit Tests (fast; many):</p><ul><li>Test individual tools in isolation (mock LLM; verify tool logic)</li><li>Test prompt templates (given context; verify correct prompt construction)</li><li>Test parsers (given LLM output; verify correct extraction)</li></ul><p>Level 2 — Integration Tests (medium speed):</p><ul><li>Test complete agent loops with deterministic inputs</li><li>“Golden trajectory” tests: known-good execution traces that must reproduce</li><li>Tool chain tests: verify multi-tool sequences work end-to-end</li></ul><p>Level 3 — Behavioral Tests (slow; few):</p><ul><li>Does the agent follow safety constraints? (adversarial inputs)</li><li>Does it ask for clarification when appropriate?</li><li>Does it stay within token/cost budgets?</li></ul><p>Production evaluation:</p><ul><li>A/B testing: Route 5% of traffic to new agent version</li><li>Shadow mode: Run new agent alongside old; compare outputs without serving</li><li>LLM-as-judge: Automated quality scoring of agent responses</li><li>User satisfaction: Thumbs up/down; task completion rate; time-to-resolution</li></ul><p>Key metric: Task Success Rate (TSR) — fraction of tasks the agent completes correctly without human intervention.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 14 and 24 (LLM Evaluation; Agent Development Frameworks).</em></p>

<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What makes SWE-bench a particularly challenging agent benchmark?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>SWE-bench tests agents on real GitHub issues from popular Python repositories:</p><p>Why it’s hard:</p><ol><li>Repository-scale context: Agent must understand codebases with 100K+ lines. <summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Compare chat-based vs canvas-based UI paradigms for agents</summary>

<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Chat-based (ChatGPT; Claude default):</p><ul><li>Linear message stream: user <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> assistant <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> user <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> …</li><li>Pro: Familiar UX; natural for exploration and Q&amp;A; easy to implement</li><li>Con: Generated artifacts (code/documents) are buried in conversation. <details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s29-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: How do you design approval gates for human-in-the-loop agent systems?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Approval gates pause agent execution at critical points for human review.</p><p>Three-tier model:</p><ol><li>Auto-approve (no gate): Safe reversible actions. Read operations; searches; calculations.</li><li>Notify (soft gate): Potentially impactful but recoverable. Send email; create draft; modify file. Agent proceeds but user is notified and can undo.</li><li>Block (hard gate): Irreversible or high-stakes. Delete data; send money; publish content; execute code with side effects. Agent MUST wait for explicit approval.</li></ol><p>Design principles:</p><ul><li>Minimize interruptions: Too many gates = user abandons the agent. The 3-tier model lets most actions flow while catching dangerous ones.</li><li>Show context: At approval gate display: what action; why (agent’s reasoning); what will change; how to undo.</li><li>Batch approvals: If agent needs 5 file writes present them together not one by one.</li><li>Timeout handling: If user doesn’t respond within <math alttext="T" display="inline"><semantics><mi>T</mi></semantics></math> minutes either retry notification or proceed with safe default or abort gracefully.</li><li>Learning from approvals: Track approval/rejection patterns. If users always approve a certain action type consider auto-promoting it.</li></ul><p>Implementation: Tool annotations (MCP’s <code>destructiveHint</code> and <code>readOnlyHint</code>) drive automatic gate assignment. Custom rules can override based on context.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 17 and 25 (Agent Harness; Agentic UI Frameworks).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s30-p1">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Explain Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) and why it works for hybrid retrieval</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>RRF combines rankings from multiple retrieval systems without needing score calibration:</p><div class="hh-equation"><math alttext="\text{RRF}(d)=\sum_{r\in R}\frac{1}{k+r(d)}" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mtext>RRF</mtext><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>d</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow><mo rspace="0.111em">=</mo><mrow><munder><mo movablelimits="false">∑</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>∈</mo><mi>R</mi></mrow></munder><mfrac><mn>1</mn><mrow><mi>k</mi><mo>+</mo><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>d</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mfrac></mrow></mrow></semantics></math></div><p>where <math alttext="r(d)" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>r</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mi>d</mi><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></semantics></math> is the rank of document <math alttext="d" display="inline"><semantics><mi>d</mi></semantics></math> in retriever <math alttext="r" display="inline"><semantics><mi>r</mi></semantics></math>, and <math alttext="k=60" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mi>k</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>60</mn></mrow></semantics></math> is a constant that prevents high-ranked documents from dominating.</p><p>Why it works:</p><ol><li>No score normalization needed: BM25 scores are unbounded; dense similarity is in <math alttext="[-1,1]" display="inline"><semantics><mrow><mo stretchy="false">[</mo><mrow><mrow><mo>−</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mo>,</mo><mn>1</mn></mrow><mo stretchy="false">]</mo></mrow></semantics></math>. <details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s30-p2">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is Agentic RAG and how does it differ from standard RAG?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Standard RAG follows a fixed pipeline: query <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> retrieve <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> generate. It has no ability to:</p><ul><li>Decide whether retrieval is needed at all</li><li>Evaluate if retrieved documents are sufficient</li><li>Reformulate queries when retrieval fails</li><li>Combine information from multiple retrieval steps</li></ul><p>Agentic RAG treats retrieval as an <em>action</em> in the agent’s MDP:</p><ul><li>Retrieve-or-not decision: Agent assesses if it already knows the answer (skip retrieval for factual questions in its training data)</li><li>Query planning: Decomposes complex questions into sub-queries (“What year did X happen?” + “Who was president then?”)</li><li>Self-evaluation: After retrieval, grades relevance. If insufficient, reformulates query or tries different source.</li><li>Multi-hop reasoning: Retrieves <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> reasons <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> identifies knowledge gaps <math alttext="\to" display="inline"><semantics><mo stretchy="false">→</mo></semantics></math> retrieves again</li><li>Source routing: Routes queries to appropriate knowledge bases (web for current events; internal docs for company info; code search for programming)</li></ul><p>Key architectural difference: Standard RAG = deterministic pipeline. Agentic RAG = state machine with conditional transitions (LangGraph pattern with retrieve/grade/rewrite/generate nodes).</p><p>Trade-off: Agentic RAG is more accurate on complex queries but adds latency (multiple LLM calls for routing/grading). Use standard RAG for simple factual lookups; agentic RAG for multi-hop or ambiguous queries.</p><p><em>Review: Chapters 15 and 17 (RAG; Agent Harness).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s30-p3">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: Compare Self-RAG and CRAG approaches to improving retrieval quality</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023):</p><ul><li>Trains special <em>reflection tokens</em> into the LLM vocabulary</li><li>At inference, model outputs tokens like [Retrieve], [IsRel], [IsSup], [IsUse]</li><li>Model decides <em>when</em> to retrieve (not every query needs it)</li><li>After retrieval, model self-grades: Is the retrieved passage relevant? Does my answer follow from it?</li><li>Training: SFT on data augmented with reflection labels from GPT-4</li><li>Pro: Single model handles everything. Con: Requires custom training.</li></ul><p>CRAG (Corrective RAG, Yan et al., 2024):</p><ul><li>Uses a lightweight <em>retrieval evaluator</em> (separate model) to grade retrieved docs</li><li>Three actions based on confidence: <code>Correct</code> (use as-is), <code>Ambiguous</code> (augment with web search), <code>Incorrect</code> (discard; fallback to web)</li><li>Adds a <em>knowledge refinement</em> step: extract only relevant sentences from retrieved docs</li><li>Pro: Works with any frozen LLM. Con: Extra model for evaluation; added latency.</li></ul><p>Key difference: Self-RAG embeds retrieval decisions into the LLM itself (requires training). CRAG is a pipeline approach that wraps around any LLM (no training needed). Self-RAG is more elegant; CRAG is more practical for production with existing models.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 15 (Retrieval-Augmented Generation).</em></p>
</div>
</details>

<details class="hh-quiz" id="ch28-s30-p4">
<summary class="hh-quiz-question">Q: What is the lost-in-the-middle problem and how do you mitigate it?</summary>
<div class="hh-quiz-answer">
<p class="hh-quiz-answer-label">解答</p>
<p>The problem: When retrieved context is long (many passages), LLMs disproportionately attend to information at the <em>beginning</em> and <em>end</em> of the context, ignoring information in the middle. If the answer is in passage 5 of 10, the model may miss it.</p><p>Empirical evidence: Liu et al. (2023) showed that for 20-document retrieval, accuracy drops by 15–20% when the relevant document is in positions 5–15 vs positions 1–3.</p><p>Mitigation strategies:</p><ol><li>Re-rank and truncate: Use a cross-encoder to re-rank, then only include top-3 most relevant passages (fewer = less lost-in-middle).</li><li>Strategic ordering: Place highest-relevance passages at the start AND end of context, low-relevance in the middle.</li><li>Contextual compression: Summarize each passage to 1–2 sentences before insertion. Less text = less position bias.</li><li>Map-reduce: Process each passage independently (map), then combine answers (reduce). Eliminates position effects entirely.</li><li>Citation prompting: Ask model to cite which passage it used. This forces attention to all passages.</li><li>Chunk size reduction: Smaller chunks mean fewer total chunks needed to cover the answer.</li></ol><p>Best practice: Retrieve many (20+), re-rank to top 3–5, order by relevance (best first). This sidesteps the problem entirely for most use cases.</p><p><em>Review: Chapter 15 (Retrieval-Augmented Generation).</em></p>
